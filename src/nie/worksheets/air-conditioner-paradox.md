## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

A **paradox** is something that seems to work against itself. This article is about one you can plug into the wall: the air conditioner.

For about a hundred years, many people in Europe thought air conditioning was something they did not need. Now summers there are getting hotter, and people are buying air conditioners by the million. Heat waves are dangerous, especially for older, sick and poor people, and air conditioning is the best tool for keeping people safe in a bad one.

Here is the catch. Air conditioners use lots of electricity on the hottest afternoons, and in much of the world that electricity still comes from burning fossil fuels. The cooling chemicals inside, called refrigerants, also warm the planet if they leak. So David describes a **loop**: warming leads to more cooling, and cooling, the way it is powered today, leads to more warming.

David says nobody is the villain. A grandmother buying a cooling unit is making a sensible choice. This is a system problem, and he sees four ways out. Better machines: the best ones give several times more cooling for the same power as the cheap ones. Timing: cooling is needed most on sunny afternoons, just when solar panels make the most power. Buildings: shade trees, thick walls, breezes and white roofs cool a neighborhood for free. Honesty: cooling should be planned as a basic need, like heating and clean water.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses efficiency to mean how much useful work something does for the energy it uses.

**1.** Heat waves are getting worse, and air conditioning is the most effective way to keep people safe in a severe heat wave.

**2.** About a tenth.

**3.** Air conditioners use lots of electricity, and in much of the world that electricity still comes from fossil fuels. Also, the refrigerants inside them are strong greenhouse gases when they leak.

**4.** Cooling is needed most in the afternoon, and solar panels also make the most power in the afternoon. The same sun that causes the need for cooling can help power it, with batteries helping in the early evening.

**5.** Cheap air conditioners cost less at the store but use much more electricity for the same cooling, so families pay more later on their electricity bills. The cheap, inefficient machines are the ones most likely to be bought in places where price matters most, so the families with the least money can end up paying the most to stay cool, and the extra electricity adds to warming.

**Audit (sample answers):** Who builds it: companies that make the machines; governments can shape which machines are sold through efficiency standards, labeling and bulk buying; architects, city planners and heat officers shape the buildings and streets. Who pays: families pay to buy the units and then pay to run them on their electricity bills; cheap units cost less up front but more over time. Who gets left off: households least able to afford the good machines, and the older, sick and poor people who are most at risk in heat waves. The article suggests public cooling centers, efficiency help aimed at those households, and cooler streets with trees and reflective surfaces.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. For the first question, strong answers explain that trees give shade at no cost and without using electricity, so cooling can start with nature and design before any machine turns on; other ways from the article include thick walls, cross-breezes, white roofs and reflective surfaces. For the second, strong answers might include opening public cooling centers, planting street trees, helping families get efficient air conditioners, or making sure the power grid is ready for hot summers.

**Report:** Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example: heat waves are dangerous, especially for older, sick and poor people; air conditioning saves lives; cooling centers and efficiency help can protect families who cannot afford good machines), a fair statement of the other side (for example: more cooling uses more electricity and can add to warming, or planning costs money), and a conclusion that matches the answer.
