## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

Every August, many students feel a low hum of worry before school starts. Most people say they are nervous about the schoolwork. Psychology editor Gawon Ryu thinks that answer is mostly wrong.

Her clue is timing. The feeling is strongest before the first day, and it drops within a week or two of school starting. But schoolwork keeps growing all fall, so the feeling can't mainly be about work.

Gawon says what runs out each summer is your **map** of school. By June, you knew where you sat, who you sat with, and what each teacher meant. In September, you get a blank map and an old question: "Where do I fit here?" For a very long time, humans needed their group to survive, so the brain treats not knowing where you fit like a danger. The feeling does not mean something is broken. It is your mind asking for directions. Once you find your table, the map comes back.

That is why a neat binder doesn't help much. What helps is **anchor-building**: making one or two things certain ahead of time. You could talk to one classmate before the first day, walk through a new building once, get back to regular bedtimes and mealtimes, or plan something fun for the first weekend.

Gawon also sees good news. A new school year is a fresh start, a chance to try a new role or choose a new table.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

*Note: the personal-reflection prompts on this sheet are optional. Students should not be asked to share their own worries in writing.*

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses anchor to mean something steady you can count on (or the object that holds a ship in place).

**1.** Before the first day.

**2.** The feeling drops within a week or two of school starting, while the schoolwork keeps growing, so if it were about work it would get bigger, not smaller.

**3.** Any two: where you sat, who you sat with, what each teacher meant or expected, what your role was in each room, who you were at that school.

**4.** Any two: reaching out to one classmate before the first day, walking through a new building once, getting back to regular sleep and mealtimes in the last week of summer, planning something ordinary and good for the first weekend.

**5.** Gawon thinks the feeling comes from an expired social map, not from fear of the work. A binder prepares you for schoolwork, so it doesn't fix the real problem. Social anchors, like one familiar face on the first morning, make part of the new map certain before school begins.

**Mind map (sample answers):** The expired map: over the summer, your sense of where you fit at school runs out, and in September you have to work out where you belong again (e.g., not yet knowing where to sit at lunch). Anchor-building: making one or two things certain before school starts (e.g., planning to meet one friend at the door). A fresh start: a new school year is a chance to change your role or choose a new table, and people use special dates like new years and new semesters to make changes (e.g., deciding to join a new club). Surprise: answers vary; possible answers include that the feeling fades even though the work gets harder, that the brain treats not knowing where you fit like a danger, or that a single familiar face can make the first morning easier.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. For the ★ question, strong answers explain that the mind wants to know where it stands and where it fits, like someone looking for their spot on a map; helpful ideas can come from the article (one familiar classmate, walking the building). For the ★★ question, strong answers take a side and use the article: Gawon says the worry and the possibility are the same signal read at different volumes, because a blank map also means roles can change; a student who disagrees might say nervous and excited feelings are different and should be handled differently.

**Report:** Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example, the timing of the feeling and the idea of an expired map), a fair statement of the other side (for example, that people usually describe it as being nervous about schoolwork), and a conclusion that matches the answer.
