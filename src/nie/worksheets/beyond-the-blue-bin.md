## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

Many people think that sorting plastic, paper, and glass into the blue bin takes care of the trash problem. Ethan says it is not that simple.

Most things we use follow a straight line he calls "take-make-waste." We dig up raw materials, make products that don't last long, and throw them away. Recycling usually doesn't turn a plastic bottle back into a new bottle. Instead, it becomes a lower-quality material, like carpet or cloth, which gets thrown away later. This is called **downcycling**. It slows the trip to the landfill, but doesn't stop it.

In a **circular economy**, materials would be used again and again, in a loop. To get there, Ethan says we have to start when a product is first designed. Things should be built to last, easy to repair, and easy to take apart, with standard screws instead of glue, so their parts can be used again.

Some companies already do this. Patagonia repairs outdoor gear and sells used items through its Worn Wear program. Fairphone makes phones where you can replace the battery, camera, and screen yourself.

But Ethan says companies can't do it alone. He wants laws that make companies responsible for their products from start to finish, better places to sort and rebuild materials, and shoppers who care more about quality than newness. Keep recycling, he says. The real goal is a world where we barely need a blue bin at all.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses downcycling to mean recycling something into a lower-quality material.

**1.** It is the way our economy works now: we take raw materials, make them into products that don't last long, and then throw them away.

**2.** It becomes a lower-quality material, like carpet or cloth.

**3.** Any two: built to last instead of wearing out quickly; easy to repair, with parts and instructions people can get; easy to take apart, using standard screws instead of glue; made of materials that can safely go back to nature or be reused over and over.

**4.** Patagonia: its Worn Wear program repairs gear and sells used items. Fairphone: it makes phones where users can easily replace the battery, camera, and screen.

**5.** Any two: "producer responsibility" laws that make companies pay for what happens to their products for their whole life; investment in better sorting and remanufacturing facilities; a change in how people shop, valuing quality and long-lasting things over new things. Sample explanation: if companies have to pay for their products after they are thrown away, they will want to design them to last and be easy to recycle.

**Ledger (sample answers):** Win: less trash in landfills, fewer new raw materials taken, things last longer, and companies can earn customer loyalty and new money from repair and refurbishing. Cost: companies may have to pay for their products' whole life; building new sorting and remanufacturing facilities costs money; shoppers give up always getting something new. Catch: no single company can do it alone; it needs new laws, new facilities, and people changing their habits, and recycling itself is limited because materials wear down, get mixed with dirt or other stuff, and cost a lot to process.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. Strong answers describe products that last, get repaired, and get taken apart and reused, so very little ends up as trash. For the redesign, look for at least two ideas from the article: longer-lasting materials, parts that can be replaced, screws instead of glue, or materials that can be reused.

**Report:** Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article, a fair statement of the other side, and a conclusion that matches the answer.
