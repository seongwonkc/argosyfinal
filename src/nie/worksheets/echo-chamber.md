## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

People hoped social media would be like a big town square, where people from different places could share ideas and talk things over. Psychology editor Gawon Ryu says many feeds have become **echo chambers** instead: places where you mostly hear your own ideas repeated back to you.

Why? Social media companies earn money by showing ads, so they want you to stay on their apps as long as possible. Their computer programs, called algorithms, learn what keeps you scrolling. Strong feelings, especially anger, keep people watching, so feeds often fill up with emotional posts that pick sides.

This works on two habits that every brain has. The first is **confirmation bias**: we like to find and remember information that agrees with what we already believe. The algorithm learns what you believe and shows you more of it. The second is group polarization. When people who already agree only talk with each other, their views often grow more extreme. Over time, people on the other side can start to seem impossible to understand.

An echo chamber can also trick you into thinking almost everyone agrees with you. Then, when you meet someone who doesn't, it can feel shocking.

Gawon's advice is to look for different points of view and to question what your feed shows you. A feed, she says, is not a window onto the world. It is more like a mirror.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses skepticism to mean not believing something right away and checking it first.

**1.** To keep you on the app as long as possible so they can show you more ads.

**2.** Negative emotions, especially anger or outrage.

**3.** Confirmation bias is the habit of looking for, understanding, and remembering information in a way that agrees with what we already believe.

**4.** When everything in your feed agrees with you, you start to believe that almost everyone thinks the way you do, even though they don't.

**5.** The algorithm learns what you like and believe and keeps showing you content that agrees with it, which feeds your confirmation bias. Because you are always surrounded by people and posts that agree with you, it is like being in a nonstop discussion with like-minded people, and group polarization pushes your views further to the extreme. In the end, people who disagree can seem impossible to understand.

**Mind map (sample answers):** Confirmation bias: paying more attention to information that agrees with what you already think (e.g., only reading reviews that agree with your opinion of a movie). Group polarization: when people who agree only talk to each other, their opinions get stronger and more extreme (e.g., a group of fans who grow more and more sure their team is the best). Illusion of consensus: believing almost everyone agrees with you because everything you see agrees with you (e.g., being shocked that a classmate likes a show you thought everyone disliked). Surprise: answers vary; possible answers include that apps are built mainly to keep people watching rather than to inform them, that anger keeps people engaged, or that the feed learns what makes you angry.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. For the ★ question, strong answers explain that a mirror shows you yourself, so a feed mostly shows you your own beliefs instead of the whole world; ideas from the article include looking for different perspectives, following people you disagree with, and being skeptical of what the feed shows. For the ★★ question, strong answers take a side and use the article: the article says the business model is built to keep people scrolling, which could support changing the apps, but it also says each person must make a conscious effort to seek out other views.

**Report:** Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example, that algorithms show what keeps us scrolling, or that echo chambers create an illusion of consensus), a fair statement of the other side (for example, that a person can choose to follow people with different views), and a conclusion that matches the answer.
