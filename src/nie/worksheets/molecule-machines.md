## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

Tech companies have long promised that artificial intelligence (AI) will design new medicines. This year, that promise faces its first real test: AI-designed drugs are moving into human **clinical trials**.

Part of the story is already a success. Proteins are tiny parts of living things, and a protein's shape decides what job it does. For fifty years, scientists struggled to predict what shape a protein would fold into. Finding just one shape by experiment could fill a scientist's whole PhD. Then a family of AI models called AlphaFold learned to predict shapes in minutes, for almost every protein science knows. It won a Nobel Prize in chemistry.

But knowing a shape is not the same as having a medicine. A drug has to stick to the right protein, stay away from thousands of wrong ones, and survive the liver, which tries to break it down. About nine out of ten drugs that reach human trials fail, usually because the body is more tangled than scientists expected.

The real question is: can AI make better guesses, so fewer drugs fail? AI labs have now started early trials of medicines for cancer and the immune system.

Here is the catch. Early trials, called **Phase 1**, only check safety in small groups of people. Whether a drug actually works is tested years later. Ejin's score for 2026: the molecules are in the exam room, but no grades are posted yet.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses candidate to mean a possible new drug still being tested.

**1.** Predicting what shape a protein folds into from its gene sequence. Finding one shape by experiment used to take a very long time; the models did it in minutes.

**2.** 9 in 10.

**3.** Any two: stick (bind) to the right protein; avoid thousands of wrong proteins; survive the liver, which tries to break it down.

**4.** Whether the drug is safe (Phase 1 measures safety in small groups of people).

**5.** "Faster paperwork" means doing the same old drug discovery more quickly, with drugs still failing at the same rate. "Better guesses" means AI designs drugs that fail less often because it understands molecular shape.

**Scoreboard (sample answers):**

| Claim | Shown so far | Score |
|---|---|---|
| AI can design new medicines. | AI-designed candidates have entered early human trials; no results yet. | not yet |
| AI-designed drugs are safe in people. | Phase 1 safety trials are just starting. | not yet |
| AI-designed drugs will fail less often than nine in ten. | Too few candidates have been tested for the ratio to mean anything. | not yet or we don't know |
| AI can hit "undruggable" targets. | A couple of current candidates aim at such targets; no results yet. | not yet |

Accept "not yet" or "we don't know" for any drug claim if the student explains that trial results are not in. Only the protein-shape breakthrough is "proven." **Follow-up:** candidates hitting "undruggable" targets in trials, or AI-designed drugs failing clearly less often than nine in ten once enough have been tested.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. The AI drugs have started being tested ("in the exam room"), but the results that show whether they work are years away ("no grades posted"); Phase 1 only checks safety. Ejin says anyone declaring victory now is "selling something" and anyone declaring failure is "guessing." For the ★★ question, strong answers say that naming the test in advance keeps you honest: you cannot change the rules after you see the results, and readers can check whether you were right.

**Report:** The best-supported answer is "not yet." Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example: Phase 1 only checks safety; efficacy results are years away; nine in ten drug candidates fail; a protein shape is not a drug), a fair statement of the other side (for example: the protein-shape breakthrough was real and won a Nobel Prize; some candidates aim at "undruggable" targets; the industry is investing huge sums), and a conclusion that matches the answer.
