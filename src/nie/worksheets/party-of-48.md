## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

In June 2026, the soccer World Cup came to North America. For the first time, 48 teams played, which is 16 more teams than ever before. There were 104 matches in three host countries: the United States, Canada, and Mexico.

Patrick Kim says the World Cup is the biggest shared event people have. Billions of people watch it. All year, Patrick writes about the end of the **monoculture**, the days when almost everyone watched the same things. Today, most people watch different things on their own screens. The World Cup is the rare exception, when strangers gather around one screen and cheer together. Patrick calls this crowd the **assembled audience**.

Many people in the United States and Canada came from other countries, so lots of teams had fans living nearby. Some fans got their first chance to see their home country play on this stage. Patrick says the host became "everyone's second country."

Patrick also looks at the money. He says adding more teams was a business decision first: more matches meant more money. Ticket prices could change with demand, and special packages cost a lot. Many fans watched from outside the stadiums, at watch parties, in plazas, and in restaurants.

Even so, Patrick calls the World Cup a party for the whole planet. His advice: "Bring a flag. Any flag."

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses host to mean the place where an event happens (or the act of holding it).

**1.** 48 teams and 104 matches.

**2.** The United States, Canada, and Mexico.

**3.** The United States and Canada are home to people from all over the world, and Mexico has its own soccer fans, so many visiting teams were surrounded by fans from their home country who live in the host cities.

**4.** Any two of: watch parties, plazas, restaurants.

**5.** He means the main reason for adding teams was money: more teams and more matches bring more TV (media) money and keep more soccer federations happy. Purists say that more is not better, just more.

**Attention map (sample answers):** World Cup, who is watching: billions of people around the world; fans in the host cities; families whose home countries are playing, including teams playing for the first time; American fans new to the sport; strangers at the same screen. Who pays and who profits: fans pay for tickets that change price with demand, for expensive packages, and for resale tickets; the tournament earns money from TV rights and tickets; resale sellers take a cut; hotels in some cities raise their prices. What brings people together: cheering for their home country, a team from their family's history playing nearby, the excitement of one huge shared event, watch parties and plazas. Shared-thing column and comparison: answers vary; look for specific groups of watchers, a reasonable guess about who pays and earns, and a clear reason people gather.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. For the first, strong answers explain that the World Cup is no longer just a show you choose to turn on; it is all around you, in traffic reports, restaurants, and group chats, like the weather. For the second, strong answers weigh both sides: the tournament earns money from tickets, but the fans who make the World Cup special are often left outside. Look for a clear opinion with a reason.

**Report:** Look for a clear answer and two reasons from the article. Reasons for "better": 16 more nations got to play, and some fans saw their country's team on this stage for the first time. Reasons for "not better": the change was mostly about money, purists say more is not better, and the group-stage math got more confusing. Check for a fair statement of the other side and a conclusion that matches the answer.
