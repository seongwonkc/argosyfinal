## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

Quantum rules are the strange rules that tiny things, like electrons and atoms, follow. For example, a tiny particle can slip through an energy barrier, a bit like a ball rolling straight through a hill instead of over it. This is called **tunneling**. Most people believed these rules only work for very small things, never for objects big enough to see.

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics went to three scientists, John Clarke, Michel Devoret and John Martinis, who showed otherwise. In the mid-1980s, they worked with an electrical circuit made of metal and big enough to see. They showed the whole circuit tunneling, and taking in energy only in fixed steps, like climbing stairs instead of a ramp. Billions of electrons acted together as one quantum object.

The hardest part was keeping the circuit quiet. Quantum behavior is "shy": any careless bump or stray noise makes it act normal again. So the team cooled the circuit to nearly the coldest temperature possible and shielded it from every disturbance.

That circuit is the direct ancestor of the **superconducting qubit**, the part inside many of the world's most famous quantum computers. Getting from the experiment to those computers took forty years.

Ejin's lesson: a curious question with no money plan grew into a whole industry. But today's quantum computers still make many errors and are not yet useful products. The prize honors the real foundation, not the hype.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses isolate to mean keeping something separate and protected from its surroundings.

**1.** That a whole electrical circuit, big enough to see, could follow quantum rules: it tunneled through an energy barrier and took in energy in fixed (discrete) steps, with billions of electrons acting as one quantum object.

**2.** 40 years.

**3.** Isolating the circuit: cooling it to near absolute zero and shielding it from stray noise. It mattered because quantum behavior is "shy"; if anything disturbs the circuit carelessly, it goes back to behaving normally (classically).

**4.** Scientific instruments that still make many errors.

**5.** In the 1980s, the question "can a circuit be quantum?" had no product plan, no market and no clear use. Nobody could have promised honestly that it would matter decades later, so the science had to be worth doing for its own sake first.

**Scoreboard (sample answers):**

| Claim | Shown so far | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Engineers can build electrical parts that keep quantum rules. | The experiment proved circuits you can design and wire together can still tunnel. | proven |
| The 1980s circuit led to real quantum computer hardware. | It is the direct ancestor of the superconducting qubit used in famous quantum computers. | proven |
| Quantum computers will become useful machines. | Today's machines are scientific instruments; the date for a useful machine keeps sliding. | not yet or we don't know |
| Quantum computers can work without many mistakes. | The article says error rates are still "brutal." | not yet |

Accept "not yet" or "we don't know" for claims about useful quantum computers if the student explains that the machines are not yet products. Only the circuit experiment and its link to qubit hardware are "proven." **Follow-up:** error rates dropping far enough, or a quantum computer that works as a useful machine rather than a scientific instrument.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. The foundation is the real, proven science: circuits that follow quantum rules, built through forty years of careful experiments. The hype is the big promises about quantum computers, which still have high error rates and are not yet useful products. Ejin keeps them apart because a prize for the foundation does not prove the promises. For the ★★ question, common sense said quantum rules only work for tiny things and could never show up in something you can see; the three physicists refused to believe the quantum world had a size limit, and their experiment proved them right.

**Report:** The best-supported answer is "no" or "not yet." Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example: today's quantum computers are scientific instruments, not products; error rates are still brutal; the date for a useful machine keeps sliding; the Nobel honors the foundation, not the hype), a fair statement of the other side (for example: the circuit experiment is proven and won a Nobel; it led directly to the qubits in famous quantum computers; governments now treat quantum computing as important infrastructure), and a conclusion that matches the answer.
