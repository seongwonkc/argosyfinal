## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

Some big moments get a ceremony, with ribbons and cameras. This one got a spreadsheet. The yearly reviews of the world's electricity data showed that **renewable energy** has passed coal as the biggest source of electricity on Earth. In 2025, renewables made about a third of the world's electricity, just a little more than coal.

That is a big deal. Coal has powered the world's electricity since electricity began. In the past, new kinds of energy were simply added on top: oil joined coal, gas joined oil, and nothing actually shrank. This time, electricity from **fossil fuels** started to go down, even while the world used more electricity overall.

What caused it? Solar power got cheap. For twenty years it kept getting cheaper, until in most of the world it became the cheapest electricity people have ever made. David says this was won by factory engineers, by installers, and by contests where the lowest price won, not by speeches. A price, he writes, does not get tired.

There is fine print. Electricity is only about a fifth of the energy the world uses, and steel, cement, planes, ships and heating still mostly run on fossil fuels. The sun sets every night, so until batteries get cheaper, fossil plants still fill the gaps. And the world keeps using more electricity, so clean power has to keep racing to stay ahead.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses milestone to mean an important moment that shows progress.

**1.** Renewable energy has overtaken coal as the largest source of electricity on Earth.

**2.** About a third.

**3.** In earlier transitions, new fuels were added on top of old ones and nothing actually declined. This time, fossil-fuel electricity began to go down even while total demand for electricity grew.

**4.** Solar power got cheap. It kept getting cheaper for two decades until, across most of the world, it became the cheapest electricity ever produced.

**5.** Electricity is only one part of the energy the world uses, about a fifth of it. Cleaning up the power grid is the easiest part; much of the rest still runs on fossil fuels. Things named in the article: steel, cement, planes, ships and heating.

**Audit (sample answers):** Who builds it: factory engineers who kept cutting the cost of panels, installers who learned to mount panels in hours instead of days, and countries that held auctions where the lowest bid won. Who pays: buyers chose the cheapest power, and in sunny countries the lowest bid kept being solar; as more solar was built, its price kept falling. Who gets left off: the four-fifths of energy that is not electricity, such as steel, cement, planes, ships and heating; and the nighttime hours, when the sun is down and fossil plants still fill the gap until batteries get cheaper. Also, growing demand from electric cars, heating, air conditioning and data centers means clean power has to keep up every year.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. For the first question, strong answers explain that just as gas-lit cities seem strange to us now, a world where most electricity came from burning coal may seem strange to future kids. For the second, strong answers explain that a low price keeps working whatever happens in politics, because people choose the cheaper option; reasons people still need plans and rules might include that the article says policy can speed the change up or slow it down by years, and that the fine print (energy beyond electricity, nighttime power, growing demand) still has to be solved.

**Report:** Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example: coal had powered electricity since it began; this is the first time a fuel has actually declined instead of just being added to; solar became the cheapest electricity ever), a fair statement of the other side (for example: electricity is only about a fifth of energy use; fossil plants still cover the hours when the sun is down; demand keeps rising), and a conclusion that matches the answer.
