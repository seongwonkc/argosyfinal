## 2. The Short Version

*Read this first. Older readers: read the full article too.*

When a company "goes public," it sells small pieces of itself, called **shares**, that anyone can buy. This first sale is called an **IPO**. In June 2026, the most famous rocket company went public, in what the article calls the largest IPO ever recorded. Each share has a price that changes all the time, and in its first week the company's price jumped up and down wildly.

Ejin does not cover stock prices. Ejin covers what happens to a group of people when something starts measuring them every single day.

This company became the best in its field by blowing up test rockets on purpose, in public. Its early tries at landing rockets ended in fireballs, again and again. But each crash gave the engineers new information, and now landings are so normal that TV broadcasts cut away from them. Ejin calls this buying knowledge with visible failure.

Here is the worry. Older aerospace companies became very careful, because being judged on short-term numbers punished risky, visible tests. Nothing blew up, but nothing improved either.

Here is the hope. Special voting shares keep the big decisions in the founder's hands, no matter what the price does.

So Ejin's question is: can the company keep exploding test rockets on purpose when every explosion gets a same-day price? Ejin's test is simple. Watch whether the test flights keep sometimes blowing up. The answer will take years.

## For Teachers and Parents: Answer Key

**Word bank sentence:** Answers vary; check that the student uses prototype to mean an early test version of something, built to learn from.

**1.** The company sells shares (small pieces of itself) to the public for the first time, so anyone can buy them.

**2.** It failed many times and learned from each failure.

**3.** Each explosion gives the engineers data. The data adds up over time, and that is how the company made rocket landings routine.

**4.** No rocket news at all. Nothing about the rockets changed during that week.

**5.** Pessimist: older aerospace companies became cautious because short-term measurement punished visible risk, so the same thing may eventually happen to this company. Optimist: special voting shares keep decisions with the founder, so the stock price cannot control how the engineers work.

**Scoreboard (sample answers):**

| Claim | Shown so far | Score |
|---|---|---|
| The company will keep testing boldly now that it is public. | The article says "grades take years." | not yet |
| Special voting shares will protect the engineering culture from the stock price. | The price swung with no rocket news; the article does not say whether the company's work pace changed. | not yet or we don't know |
| Becoming public will make the company cautious, like older aerospace companies. | That happened to older companies, but it is too early to tell for this one. | we don't know |
| A stock chart shows how good the rockets are. | The price soared and slid while nothing about the rockets changed. | we don't know (accept answers saying the first week argues against it) |

Accept "not yet" or "we don't know" for any claim about the company's future if the student explains that the results will take years. Only the learn-by-failing method is "proven." **Follow-up:** the test flights keep sometimes blowing up and the company keeps sharing its failures, year after year, while it is public.

**Think deeper:** Open answers. "Buying knowledge with visible failure" means paying for lessons with failures that everyone can see, like the early landing fireballs that taught engineers how to land rockets. For the ★★ question, strong answers give a clear example (for example, a team that only counts goals may stop practicing defense) and connect it to the article: if the company is scored by its share price every day, it may start avoiding visible failures, even useful ones.

**Report:** The best-supported answer is "we don't know yet." Ejin says the scoreboard "opens now" and "grades take years." Look for a clear answer, two reasons taken from the article (for example: older aerospace companies became cautious under short-term measurement; every explosion now gets a same-day price; special voting shares keep decisions with the founder; the price moved with no rocket news), a fair statement of the other side, and a conclusion that matches the answer. Accept "yes" or "no" if the student supports it with the article.
