Governments have been toppled by armies, by parliaments, by palace intrigues, and by mobs. This month, in Nepal, one was toppled by roughly the demographic that staffs this newspaper.

The sequence was stunning in its speed. In early September, Nepal's government moved to ban major social media platforms that had failed to register with the state—a move officials framed as regulatory housekeeping and the young read, correctly, as an attempt to switch off the room where they talk. Within days, protests organized largely online under banners of the "Gen Z" movement filled the streets of Kathmandu. The state answered with lethal force, and the deaths of young protesters transformed a demonstration into an uprising. By September 9, Prime Minister K.P. Sharma Oli had resigned. By September 12, an interim government was being formed under former Chief Justice Sushila Karki—the first woman to lead Nepal—with parliament dissolved and fresh elections set for March.

A ban, a massacre of trust, a resignation, a caretaker: one week, start to finish.

It is tempting for outside observers to file this under the familiar category of instability in a poor, politically turbulent country—Nepal has had more than a dozen governments since its monarchy fell in 2008. That would be a mistake. What happened in Kathmandu is better understood as a preview, and its logic reaches far beyond the Himalayas.

Consider first what the protesters were actually angry about. The social media ban was the spark, but the fuel had been accumulating for years, and it had a name young Nepalis used with acid precision: the "nepo kids." Viral posts had long contrasted the luxury goods and foreign vacations of politicians' children with the prospects of ordinary youth, in a country where unemployment pushes hundreds of thousands abroad each year to work construction in the Gulf or security jobs in Malaysia. The remittances they send home keep the economy standing. The grievance, in other words, was not ideological. It was generational and material: a gerontocracy of recycled leaders, taking turns in power for decades, presiding over an economy whose main export is its own young people.

Consider next how the movement worked. It had no party. It had no visible leadership hierarchy. When the government fell and someone had to be chosen to lead the interim arrangement, portions of the deliberation happened through online polling and forums—young citizens, having removed a government via the networks the state tried to ban, then used those same networks to negotiate what should come next. Political scientists will spend years on that detail. A leaderless movement is famously good at destruction and famously bad at what follows; Nepal's youth seem to know the history and are improvising an answer in real time.

And consider, finally, the regional echo. Nepal joins a lengthening list—Sri Lanka in 2022, Bangladesh in 2024, where a student movement ended fifteen years of Sheikh Hasina's rule—of South Asian governments brought down by their young. Each case is distinct, but the pattern is not subtle. Across the region, median ages sit in the twenties while ruling elites sit in their sixties and seventies. The young are connected, multilingual, aware of exactly how their peers live elsewhere, and locked out of both prosperity and power at home. Where institutions offer no channel for that energy, the street becomes the channel, and the smartphone becomes the organizing committee.

Here is the realist's assessment. First, the ban was not merely a policy error; it was a category error. Nepal's rulers treated social media as a broadcast system they could unplug. For their citizens under thirty, it is closer to civil society itself—the marketplace, the assembly hall, the notice board, and the pub. Banning it did not silence the conversation. It moved the conversation outdoors.

Second, the hard part begins now, and history counsels caution more than celebration. An interim government of respected elders, a dissolved parliament, elections promised for spring—these are the fragile months in which uprisings are consolidated or stolen. The old parties have not vanished; they are waiting, organized and patient, for the movement's energy to dissipate. Whether Nepal's Gen Z can convert a week of fury into durable institutions—candidates, platforms, ballots—will decide whether September was a revolution or merely a spasm. The spring election will tell us.

But third, and most broadly: every government on Earth with a young population and an old elite should be reading Kathmandu like a weather report. The specific grievances vary by country. The structure—connected youth, closed politics, conspicuous nepotism, and a state that mistakes the network for a toy it can confiscate—is alarmingly common. Nepal's rulers discovered that the group chat is now a branch of government, unofficial but sovereign in its own way.

They will not be the last to learn it.
