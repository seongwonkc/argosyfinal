Governments have been toppled by armies, by parliaments, by palace intrigues, and by mobs. This month, in Nepal, one was toppled by citizens scarcely older than the students who staff this newspaper.

The sequence rewards a sober retelling. In early September, Nepal's government, declaring that major social media platforms had failed to register with the state, moved to ban them; officials framed the measure as regulatory housekeeping, and the young read it, correctly, as an attempt to switch off the room where they talk. Within days, protests organized largely online under the banners of the "Gen Z" movement had filled the streets of Kathmandu. The state answered with lethal force, and the deaths of young protesters transformed a demonstration into an uprising. By September 9, Prime Minister K.P. Sharma Oli had resigned. By September 12, an interim government was being formed under former Chief Justice Sushila Karki, the first woman to lead Nepal, with parliament dissolved and fresh elections set for March.

A ban, a massacre of trust, a resignation, a caretaker: one week, start to finish.

The temptation, for outside observers, is to file the episode under the familiar heading of instability in a poor and turbulent country; Nepal has cycled through more than a dozen governments since its monarchy fell in 2008. The temptation should be resisted. What happened in Kathmandu is better understood as a preview, and its logic reaches far beyond the Himalayas.

The grievance came first, and it was not ideological. The social media ban was the spark; the fuel had been accumulating for years, and it bore a name that young Nepalis pronounced with acid precision: the "nepo kids." Viral posts had long set the luxury goods and foreign vacations of politicians' children against the prospects of ordinary youth, in a country where unemployment pushes hundreds of thousands abroad each year to pour concrete in the Gulf or stand guard in Malaysia, and where the remittances they send home keep the economy standing. The complaint, reduced to its elements, was generational and material: a gerontocracy of recycled leaders, taking turns in power for decades, presiding over an economy whose principal export is its own young people.

So much for the fuel. The mechanism of the movement is the stranger fact. It had no party; it had no visible leadership hierarchy; and when the government fell and someone had to be chosen to head the interim arrangement, portions of the deliberation ran through online polls and forums, so that young citizens who had removed a government by means of the networks the state tried to ban then used those same networks to negotiate what should come next. Political scientists will spend years on that detail. A leaderless movement is famously good at destruction and famously bad at what follows; Nepal's young appear to know the history, and to be improvising an answer in real time.

The regional echo completes the picture. Sri Lanka fell to its young in 2022; Bangladesh followed in 2024, when a student movement ended fifteen years of Sheikh Hasina's rule; Nepal now lengthens the list of South Asian governments brought down by the generation they neglected. Each case is distinct, but the pattern is not subtle. Across the region, median ages sit in the twenties while ruling elites sit in their sixties and seventies; the young are connected, multilingual, and precisely informed about how their peers live elsewhere, yet locked out of both prosperity and power at home. Where institutions offer no channel for that energy, the street becomes the channel, and the smartphone becomes the organizing committee.

Three conclusions follow. The first is that the ban was not merely a policy error but a category error. Nepal's rulers treated social media as a broadcast system that could be unplugged; for their citizens under thirty it is closer to civil society itself, the marketplace and the assembly hall and the notice board at once. Banning it did not silence the conversation. It moved the conversation outdoors.

The second conclusion is that the hard part begins now, and that history counsels caution more than celebration. An interim government of respected elders, a dissolved parliament, elections promised for spring: these are the fragile months in which uprisings are consolidated or stolen. The old parties have not vanished; they are waiting, organized and patient, for the movement's energy to dissipate. Whether Nepal's Gen Z can convert a week of fury into the durable furniture of politics — candidates, platforms, ballots — will determine whether September was a revolution or merely a spasm, and the spring election will render the first verdict.

The third conclusion is the broadest. Europe learned in 1848 how quickly revolt travels along the newest channels of its age, then the railway and the pamphlet, now the feed and the group chat; every government on Earth that pairs a young population with an old elite should therefore be reading Kathmandu the way sailors read a falling barometer. The particular grievances vary by country. The structure, which joins connected youth to closed politics, conspicuous nepotism, and a state that mistakes the network for a toy it can confiscate, is alarmingly common. Nepal's rulers have discovered that the group chat is now a branch of government, unofficial but sovereign in its own way.

They will not be the last to learn it.
