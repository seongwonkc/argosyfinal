It usually starts innocently. You open your phone to check one thing—a message, a score, tomorrow's weather. Forty-five minutes later, you are still there, thumb moving in the same hypnotic rhythm, absorbing a stream of bad news that stretches on without end. A war here, a disaster there, an argument about all of it in the comments. You feel worse than when you started. You keep going anyway.

This behavior has a name—doomscrolling—and it has become one of the defining habits of our era. It is tempting to describe it as a personal failure, a simple lack of self-control. But that framing misses the point. Doomscrolling is not a glitch in our psychology. It is our psychology, working exactly as it evolved to work, inside an environment that was engineered to exploit it.

**The Negativity Bias: Why Bad News Feels Important**

The human brain is not a neutral processor of information. For our ancestors, the cost of missing a threat—a predator, a rival, a poisoned berry—was catastrophically higher than the cost of missing something pleasant. Evolution responded by building in what psychologists call the negativity bias: negative information grabs our attention faster, holds it longer, and is remembered better than positive information.

In a world of scarce information, this bias was a survival tool. In a world of infinite information, it becomes a trap. Every algorithmic feed learns, within days, that we linger on alarming headlines longer than reassuring ones. The feed does not need to understand why. It simply serves more of what holds us, and what holds us is the threat. We are, in effect, paying attention the way our ancestors did—except the savannah now has no edge, and the predators never stop coming.

**The Illusion of Preparedness**

If doomscrolling made us feel terrible and nothing else, we might quit. But it offers something in return, and understanding that reward is the key to understanding the habit. Psychologists point to a deep human need for certainty and control, especially in chaotic times. When the world feels unstable, monitoring the threat feels like doing something about it.

This is the illusion of preparedness: the sense that if I just read one more article, one more thread, one more expert's take, I will finally understand the situation well enough to be safe from it. The scroll becomes a vigil. But the feeling of control it provides is counterfeit. Reading a fortieth post about a crisis on another continent does not prepare you for anything; it simply re-triggers the alarm. The behavior that promises to resolve our anxiety is the very thing renewing it.

**The Variable Reward: The Slot Machine in Your Pocket**

The third ingredient is the oldest trick in behavioral psychology. In the 1950s, B.F. Skinner discovered that animals press a lever most compulsively not when the reward is guaranteed, but when it is unpredictable. This is called a variable ratio reinforcement schedule, and it is the same mechanism that makes slot machines the most addictive game in the casino.

An infinite feed is a variable reward machine. Most of what you scroll past is forgettable, but every so often—unpredictably—something lands: a genuinely important update, a hilarious post, a comment that says exactly what you were thinking. That intermittent jackpot keeps the thumb moving through all the misery in between. The doom is the content, but the scroll is the game.

**Breaking the Loop**

None of this means we should stop paying attention to the world. Being informed is a civic responsibility, and looking away from suffering entirely is its own kind of failure. The goal is not ignorance; it is intention.

The research on habit change suggests the most effective interventions are structural, not heroic. Willpower loses to design, so change the design: move the apps off your home screen, set an actual time to read the news and an actual time to stop, and—most powerfully—replace the infinite feed with finite formats. A newspaper ends. A briefing ends. A podcast episode ends. The feed never does, and a finite mind should be suspicious of anything infinite.

Perhaps the most useful question to ask, mid-scroll, is the simplest one: is this information I am using, or information that is using me? The answer, most nights, is enough to put the phone down.
