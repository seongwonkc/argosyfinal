It was late, and I opened my phone to check one thing. A message, a score, tomorrow's weather; forty-five minutes later I was still there, thumb moving in the same hypnotic rhythm, absorbing a stream of bad news that stretched on without end. A war here, a disaster there, an argument about all of it in the comments. I felt worse than when I started. I kept going anyway.

You've felt this. Nearly everyone has: the behavior even has a name now, doomscrolling, and it has become one of the defining habits of our era. The tempting explanation is personal failure, a simple lack of self-control (my own working theory about myself for an embarrassingly long time). But I think that framing misses what is actually happening. Doomscrolling is not a glitch in our psychology. It is our psychology, working exactly as it evolved to work, inside an environment engineered to exploit it.

**Why Does Bad News Feel So Important?**

The human brain is not a neutral processor of information. For our ancestors, the cost of missing a threat — a predator, a rival, a poisoned berry — was catastrophically higher than the cost of missing something pleasant. Evolution responded by building in what psychologists call the negativity bias: negative information grabs our attention faster, holds it longer, and is remembered better than positive information.

In a world of scarce information, this bias was a survival tool. In a world of infinite information, it becomes a trap. Every algorithmic feed learns, within days, that we linger on alarming headlines longer than reassuring ones. The feed does not need to understand why. It serves more of whatever holds us, and what holds us is the threat. You and I are paying attention the way our ancestors did, except the savannah no longer has an edge, and the predators never stop coming.

**What Is the Scroll Promising You?**

If doomscrolling made us feel terrible and offered nothing back, we would quit. It offers something, and the reward is, I think, the key to the habit. Psychologists point to a deep human need for certainty and control, especially in chaotic times. When the world feels unstable, monitoring the threat feels like doing something about it.

I keep thinking about what the scroll becomes on those nights, which is a vigil. This is the illusion of preparedness: the sense that one more article, one more thread, one more expert's take, and you will finally understand the situation well enough to be safe from it. It sounds so reasonable from the inside. It sounded reasonable to me last night. And yet the fortieth post about a crisis on another continent does not prepare you for anything (there is no quiz coming, whatever the feeling insists); it re-triggers the alarm, which calls for more monitoring, which re-triggers the alarm. The promise renews itself because it never gets kept: the behavior that offers to resolve your anxiety is the very thing sustaining it, and the vigil keeps you up precisely because it works so badly.

**Why Isn't Feeling Awful Enough to Make You Stop?**

The third ingredient is the oldest trick in behavioral psychology. In the 1950s, B.F. Skinner discovered that animals press a lever most compulsively not when the reward is guaranteed, but when it is unpredictable. This is called a variable ratio reinforcement schedule, and it is the same mechanism that makes slot machines the most addictive game in the casino.

An infinite feed is a variable reward machine. Most of what you scroll past is forgettable, but every so often, unpredictably, something lands: a genuinely important update, a hilarious post, a comment that says exactly what you were thinking. That intermittent jackpot keeps the thumb moving through all the misery in between. The doom is the content. The scroll is the game.

**So What Actually Helps?**

None of this means we should stop paying attention to the world. Being informed is a civic responsibility, and looking away from suffering entirely is its own kind of failure. The goal is not ignorance; it is intention.

The research on habit change suggests the effective interventions are structural, not heroic, which I find both humbling and oddly kind (it means all that losing was never a verdict on you). Willpower loses to design, so the design is the thing to change: the apps come off the home screen, the news gets an actual time to be read and an actual time to end, and, most powerfully, the infinite feed gets swapped for finite formats. A newspaper ends. A briefing ends. A podcast episode ends. The feed never does, and I think a finite mind is entitled to be suspicious of anything infinite.

Maybe the most useful question to ask, mid-scroll, is also the simplest one: is this information I am using, or information that is using me? I asked it last night, forty-five minutes later than I should have, and it still worked. The phone went down. Yours probably will too.
