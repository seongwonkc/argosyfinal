Two years and one week after the October 7th attacks, something happened that had begun to feel impossible: the war stopped.

On October 13, the last twenty living hostages held in Gaza came home, exchanged for Palestinian prisoners, as a ceasefire brokered under an American twenty-point plan took hold. In Sharm el-Sheikh, world leaders gathered to co-sign the framework with the theatrical solemnity reserved for moments history might actually remember. Israeli forces pulled back to an agreed line inside the Strip. After two years of a war that killed on a scale that numbed the world's capacity for shock, displaced nearly the entire population of Gaza, poisoned politics on every continent, and triggered exchanges of fire from Lebanon to Yemen to Iran—quiet.

This newspaper's roundtable sat down days after October 7th, in 2023, and struggled, like everyone, to see any exit. It is worth being honest now, in the same pages: this ceasefire is a real achievement. It is also an extraordinarily fragile one. Both things are true, and the analysis that follows tries to hold them together.

**Why It Happened Now**

Wars end when the combatants' theories of victory expire. Hamas's theory—that the war would ignite a regional conflagration forcing Israel's isolation—produced immense suffering but not that outcome; by this autumn it was a shattered organization governing rubble, its remaining leverage consisting almost entirely of the hostages. Israel's theory—"total victory"—collided with the arithmetic that the final hostages could not be freed by force, and with an exhaustion, domestic and international, that even its staunchest ally began to reflect.

Into that mutual exhaustion stepped an American administration that made ending the war a personal, branded project, applying blunt pressure on both parties and recruiting the region's mediators—Egypt, Qatar, Turkey—to deliver Hamas's assent. The plan's first phase was deliberately simple, almost minimalist: hostages out, prisoners exchanged, guns silent, aid in, forces back to a line. Simplicity is why it worked. Every previous framework died of its own ambition, trying to settle the war's hardest questions before stopping it. This one stopped the war and postponed the questions.

**What Was Actually Agreed—and What Wasn't**

That design choice is also the ceasefire's central weakness, and no serious observer should look away from it. The postponed questions are not details; they are the war itself, in latent form. Who governs Gaza? The plan sketches a technocratic administration under international supervision—a "Board of Peace" in the plan's own grandiose vocabulary—but sketches are not governments. Who holds the guns? Hamas's disarmament is stipulated in principle and entirely unresolved in practice; no armed movement in history has handed over its arsenal simply because a document requested it. Who pays for and directs reconstruction, on what timeline, under whose security guarantee? And behind everything, the question that has outlasted every war and every ceasefire for three-quarters of a century: what political future, if any, is on offer to the Palestinians?

A ceasefire that defers such questions is not a peace. It is a pause with paperwork—a scaffold on which a peace could conceivably be built, by leaders with the patience and courage to build it, or from which the next war can be conveniently launched.

**The Regional Reordering**

Step back, and the strategic map has been redrawn regardless of what comes next. Iran's "axis of resistance," the network that was supposed to encircle Israel, has been mauled in every theater. The Gulf states that formally recognized Israel in recent years—and the wave of Western governments that recognized Palestine at the United Nations just weeks ago—have together transformed the diplomatic geometry: normalization and Palestinian statehood are now both live currencies in the same marketplace. And the United States, whose retreat from the region was announced by a decade of think-pieces, has just demonstrated that it remains the only power capable of imposing an outcome here—for better and for worse.

**A Realist's Closing Ledger**

The realist tradition I write from is suspicious of moments like Sharm el-Sheikh: the handshakes, the signatures, the talk of historic dawns. Documents do not hold ceasefires. Interests do—and, occasionally, the sheer human exhaustion of societies that cannot bear more. Both currently point toward the quiet holding, unevenly and with violations, for some time.

But I want to end with the part the realist ledger records last and should not. Twenty families are whole again. A population that has known two years of bombardment slept, this week, under a sky that was merely dark. Whatever comes—and the hard history of this conflict counsels against euphoria—those are not small entries. Wars are numbers until the day they stop, when you remember they were always people. The guns are quiet. The work of making that sentence permanent has not yet begun.
