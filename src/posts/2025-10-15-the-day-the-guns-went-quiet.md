Two years and one week after the October 7th attacks, something happened that had begun to feel impossible: the war stopped.

On October 13, as a ceasefire brokered under an American twenty-point plan took hold, the last twenty living hostages held in Gaza came home, exchanged for Palestinian prisoners. In Sharm el-Sheikh, world leaders gathered to co-sign the framework with the theatrical solemnity reserved for moments history might actually remember; Israeli forces pulled back to an agreed line inside the Strip. And after two years of a war that killed on a scale that numbed the world's capacity for shock, that displaced nearly the entire population of Gaza, that poisoned politics on every continent, and that drew exchanges of fire from Lebanon to Yemen to Iran, there was quiet.

This newspaper's roundtable convened days after October 7th, in 2023, and struggled, like everyone, to see any exit. Honesty in the same pages now requires a sentence whose two clauses must not be separated: the ceasefire is a real achievement, and it is an extraordinarily fragile one. The analysis that follows carries both clauses at once.

Wars end when the combatants' theories of victory expire. Hamas's theory, that the war would ignite a regional conflagration forcing Israel's isolation, produced immense suffering but not that outcome; by this autumn it was a shattered organization governing rubble, its remaining leverage consisting almost entirely of the hostages. Israel's theory of "total victory" collided with the immovable fact that the final hostages could not be freed by force, and with an exhaustion, domestic and international, that even its staunchest ally had begun to reflect.

Into that mutual exhaustion stepped an American administration that made ending the war a personal, branded project, applying blunt pressure to both parties and recruiting the region's mediators, Egypt, Qatar, and Turkey, to deliver Hamas's assent. The plan's first phase was deliberately simple, almost minimalist: hostages out, prisoners exchanged, guns silent, aid in, forces back to a line. The simplicity is why it worked. Every previous framework died of its own ambition, attempting to settle the war's hardest questions before stopping it; this one stopped the war and postponed the questions.

So much for why it happened. The harder matter is what was postponed, for the postponed questions are not details; they are the war itself, in latent form. Who governs Gaza? The plan sketches a technocratic administration under international supervision, a "Board of Peace" in its own grandiose vocabulary, but sketches are not governments. Who holds the guns? Hamas's disarmament is stipulated in principle and entirely unresolved in practice; no armed movement in history has surrendered its arsenal because a document requested it. Who pays for and directs reconstruction, on what timeline, under whose security guarantee? And behind everything stands the question that has outlasted every war and every ceasefire here for three-quarters of a century: what political future, if any, is on offer to the Palestinians?

A ceasefire that defers such questions is not a peace. It is a pause with paperwork — a scaffold on which a peace could conceivably be built, by leaders with the patience and the courage to build it, or from which the next war can be conveniently launched. The nearest precedent is the one signed on our own peninsula in 1953: an armistice that was likewise meant to precede a settlement, and that is still waiting for it.

Viewed from enough distance, meanwhile, the strategic map has been redrawn regardless of what follows. Iran's "axis of resistance," the network that was supposed to encircle Israel, has been mauled in every theater. The Gulf states that formally recognized Israel in recent years, and the wave of Western governments that recognized Palestine at the United Nations just weeks ago, have together transformed the diplomatic geometry, so that normalization and Palestinian statehood now trade as live currencies in the same marketplace. And the United States, whose retreat from the region a decade of think-pieces had announced, has just demonstrated that it remains the only power capable of imposing an outcome here, for better and for worse.

The realist tradition in which this column works is suspicious of moments like Sharm el-Sheikh, of handshakes and signatures and talk of historic dawns. Documents do not hold ceasefires. Interests do; so, occasionally, does the sheer exhaustion of societies that cannot bear more. Both currently point toward the quiet holding, unevenly and with violations, for some time.

The ledger keeps its most important entries for last, and they should not be skipped. Twenty families are whole again. A population that has known two years of bombardment slept, this week, under a sky that was merely dark. Whatever comes, and the hard history of this conflict counsels against euphoria, those are not small entries; wars are numbers until the day they stop, when it becomes plain that they were always people. The guns are quiet. The work of making that sentence permanent has not yet begun.
