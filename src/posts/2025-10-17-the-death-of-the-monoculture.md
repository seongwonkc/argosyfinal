Ask your parents what they watched on television growing up, and something strange happens: they answer instantly, and so does everyone else their age. The same handful of shows, the same theme songs, the same finales watched on the same night by what seems, in retrospect, like the entire country. When M*A*S*H ended in 1983, more than a hundred million Americans watched it together, at the same hour, on the same channel. That wasn't just a big audience. It was a shared national moment, scheduled in advance.

Now try the same question on yourself. What is the show everyone you know is watching? Not a show some people are watching—the show. The one your teachers, your teammates, your group chats, and your dentist could all discuss. If you're struggling to name one, that's not because culture got worse. It's because the monoculture—the era when a society consumed roughly the same cultural diet at roughly the same time—is dead. And its death is quietly reshaping how we relate to each other.

**How the Monoculture Worked**

The monoculture was never really a cultural achievement. It was a distribution accident. For most of the twentieth century, the pipes were scarce: three or four television networks, a few dozen radio stations, one local cineplex, one Top 40 chart. Scarcity forced aggregation. If there are only three channels, the biggest show on any of them is, by definition, enormous. Everyone watched the same things for the same reason everyone drank from the same well—there was one well.

The internet blew up the well and replaced it with an ocean. Streaming services release more scripted television in a year than the old networks did in a decade. Music no longer funnels through radio programmers; it seeps through playlists, gaming soundtracks, and fifteen-second video clips. The algorithm hands each of us a personalized channel of one. The pipes are infinite now, and infinite pipes mean no chokepoint, and no chokepoint means no moment when a hundred million people are forced to face the same direction.

**What We Gained**

Let's be fair to the present: the monoculture was also a monopoly on attention, and monopolies are rarely kind to the margins. The old system had room for a narrow band of stories, faces, and sounds—chosen by a small number of executives who looked remarkably alike. If your taste, your language, or your community fell outside the band, culture was something that happened to other people.

The fragmentation changed that. A Korean drama can top the charts in Kansas. A niche genre can sustain a scene of a few hundred thousand devoted fans, invisible to the mainstream and perfectly happy about it. An artist can build a career without ever passing through a gatekeeper's office. The long tail is real, and for the people who live in it, the death of the monoculture felt less like a loss and more like a jailbreak.

**What We Lost**

And yet. Culture is not only entertainment; it is social infrastructure. Shared stories are how strangers find common ground—the small talk that becomes real talk, the reference that tells you a person is, in some loose sense, from the same world as you. The monoculture manufactured that common ground at industrial scale. Every water-cooler conversation about last night's episode was, secretly, a small act of social maintenance.

Fragmented culture produces the opposite: a thousand deep communities with almost no bridges between them. You can know everything about your corner—every lore detail, every inside joke—and nothing about your neighbor's. Increasingly, the only cultural events that reach everyone are the ones too big to ignore: a World Cup, a viral scandal, an election. It may not be a coincidence that as the shared middle of culture hollowed out, our public conversations started feeling like collisions between groups that lack even a common vocabulary of references. We didn't just stop watching the same shows. We stopped rehearsing the skill of caring about the same thing at the same time.

**The Occasional Resurrection**

The monoculture does still flicker back to life, and the moments when it does are revealing. A stadium tour becomes a regional economic event. A film release becomes a costume party. A song becomes inescapable for one strange month. Notice what these have in common: they are events, not just content. They give people a reason to participate simultaneously, in public, together. Scarcity of time has replaced scarcity of channels as the thing that gathers us.

That suggests the monoculture didn't die because we stopped wanting shared moments. It died because sharing stopped being the default and became a choice. The audiences are still capable of assembling; they just have to be summoned.

So perhaps the task now—for artists, and honestly for the rest of us—is to take the summoning seriously. Pick your moments. Show up for the communal ones. Watch the thing while everyone else is watching it, once in a while, on purpose. A culture where togetherness is optional doesn't have to become a culture where it's rare. But it will, unless we treat common ground as something worth scheduling.
