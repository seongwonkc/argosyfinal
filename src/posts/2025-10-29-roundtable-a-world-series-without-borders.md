*Editor's Note: Welcome to our monthly roundtable. With the World Series between the Los Angeles Dodgers and the Toronto Blue Jays underway — the Fall Classic's first trip to Canada in a generation — our sports editor convened the table mid-series. We are also delighted to welcome two new staff writers, Patrick Kim and David Lee, to their first roundtable. This transcript has been lightly edited for length and clarity.*

**Yonghyuk Choi:** So we're gathered somewhere between games, and I want to start with the obvious: this is not a normal World Series. It's the defending champions — a Dodgers roster that might be the most expensive collection of talent ever assembled — against the Blue Jays, who are carrying an entire country. Canada hasn't hosted a World Series since the Joe Carter era. My first question to the table: what's the story here? The superteam, or the nation?

**Minwoo Jung:** The nation, easily. I study politics, so forgive me, but watch what a World Series in Toronto actually does: a border becomes a narrative. It's "Canada's team" against the American colossus, at a moment when Canadians feel prickly about their big neighbor for reasons that have nothing to do with baseball. Sport is the one arena where a country of forty million gets to face the hegemon on exactly equal terms — nine innings, same rules. That's why the whole country is watching, including people who couldn't name three players in April.

**Anthony Min:** I'll take the other half. The superteam is the story, because the superteam is the business model now. The Dodgers spent years converting financial scale into roster depth — and crucially, into global reach. Their stars aren't just players; they're markets. When Shohei Ohtani plays in late October, the audience isn't American plus Canadian, it's Japanese, Korean, global. A World Series like this is the sport's balance sheet on display: one franchise built like a multinational, one built like a national champion.

**Patrick Kim:** Since it's my first roundtable, I'll confess I come to baseball as a culture writer, not a box-score person. What fascinates me is that this series is a monoculture event — I just wrote about how rare those are. Two countries synchronizing their evenings around the same game, strangers talking about it in elevators. You can't buy that. Actually, that's my question for Anthony: can you buy that? Because the Dodgers kind of tried.

**Anthony Min:** You can buy the team. You can't buy the evening. The evening has to be earned by the games being close.

**Yonghyuk Choi:** Which they have been — this series has already delivered the kind of extra-inning chaos that ages managers a decade in a night. But let me push the uncomfortable question: is a team this good *good* for baseball? The old worry is competitive balance — that when payroll predicts the postseason, thirty fanbases become five. The counterargument is that dynasties create villains, and villains create ratings. Everyone outside Los Angeles now has a rooting interest, and it's wearing a maple leaf.

**David Lee:** Rookie observation from the other new guy: this is also the first World Series I can remember where I've heard people talk about the *time zones* as much as the teams. Games ending after midnight on the East Coast, kids asleep before the ninth inning. If the sport's future depends on new fans, it's strange to schedule your biggest moments for when they can't watch. Every league says it wants the next generation; the broadcast windows say something else.

**Minwoo Jung:** That's the realist's read of every institution, David — watch what it does, not what it says.

**Yonghyuk Choi:** Spoken like our Editor-in-Chief. Before we close: predictions? I'll go first and hedge like a professional — I think this goes the distance. The Dodgers have more ways to win, but the Blue Jays have the thing analytics can't price, which is a building that shakes.

**Anthony Min:** Dodgers, because depth beats destiny over seven games. It usually does.

**Minwoo Jung:** Toronto. Sometimes the story is just too good, and history has a weakness for good stories.

**Patrick Kim:** I predict the deciding game becomes one of those where-were-you nights. That's a coward's answer, but it's my first week.

**David Lee:** Whoever wins, I predict the loser's city is unbearable for exactly one winter and then fine.

---
### Final Thoughts

**Yonghyuk Choi:** A World Series that two countries refuse to sleep through is the best advertisement baseball has produced in years — whoever lifts the trophy.

**Anthony Min:** The Dodgers are what happens when a franchise treats championships as a product line. It is impressive, a little chilling, and coming soon to every sport near you.

**Minwoo Jung:** Nations don't get many safe places to feel like nations. A ballpark in Toronto in late October is one of them, and it's worth appreciating.

**Patrick Kim:** Shared evenings are the rarest thing in culture now. This series is manufacturing them nightly.

**David Lee:** Somewhere there's a ten-year-old sneaking a radio under the blanket for the tenth inning. The sport's whole future is that kid. Schedule accordingly.
