Two championships. Two continents. One exhausted sofa.

Last weekend was the rarest gift a baseball fan gets, and I'm still recovering. On Friday night in Daejeon, the LG Twins finished off the Hanwha Eagles to win the Korean Series. Barely a day later, in Toronto, the Los Angeles Dodgers went eleven innings to beat the Blue Jays in one of the great Game 7s ever played and became baseball's first repeat champion in a quarter century. I watched both, in two languages, on very little sleep. Worth it.

**Game 7**

Toronto first, because Game 7s like this come along once a decade. Maybe once a generation. Fine, once a decade. But a good decade. The Blue Jays carried a country's hopes deep into a November night and took a lead into the ninth with the title sitting right there. Then Miguel Rojas happened. The Dodgers' least likely hero, a veteran glove-first infielder, homering to tie it. In the eleventh, catcher Will Smith hit the shot that won it all, 5–4.

And in between, the defending champs emptied the tank in a way people will retell for years. Yoshinobu Yamamoto had already thrown a complete game earlier in the series. He came out of the bullpen anyway. No rest. Closed out the final innings. World Series MVP, three wins, Games 2, 6, and 7, a workload from another era, delivered by a pitcher in his second big-league season out of Japan. I ran out of ways to yell at my TV somewhere around the tenth.

At our roundtable last week, I predicted the series would go the distance because Toronto had "a building that shakes." It did shake. It just fell silent one swing too early. My colleague Anthony predicted the Dodgers because "depth beats destiny over seven games." I hate it when he's right. And he was precisely right: the difference in the end was that Los Angeles had one more trustworthy arm, one more professional at-bat, one more answer. That's what all that payroll actually buys. Not certainty. Answers, stacked deep enough to survive a night when destiny is swinging hard the other way.

**Meanwhile, Daejeon**

Half a world away, the Korean Series told a different story in the same sport. The LG Twins took down the Hanwha Eagles in five, and the series turned on a collapse-and-response worthy of any classic: LG coughed up a six-run eighth to lose Game 3, then answered in Game 4 with a six-run ninth of their own to steal it right back. The kind of gut-punch exchange that decides short series. The Twins' fourth title arrived through the hands of series MVP Kim Hyun-soo — a thirty-seven-year-old veteran collecting rings in the twilight of a great career. Try not smiling at that. You can't.

If you've never watched KBO baseball, the difference from MLB isn't talent. It's texture. Korean baseball is louder, more communal, more theatrical. Cheering squads. Drums. Every hitter with his own song, sung by thousands of people who never sit down. October in Jamsil or Daejeon feels less like attending a game than joining one. American baseball sells you tension in reverent silence. Korean baseball refuses to let you experience a single pitch alone.

**Two Theories**

Toggling between feeds, this is what got me. MLB's finale was a showcase of concentration. The best players from everywhere: Japan, Venezuela, Korea, everywhere. Gathered onto a few super-rosters, playing for a global audience at one in the morning. The KBO's finale was a showcase of location. A league that belongs completely to its place, tuned to its own fans, its heroes household names at home and rumors abroad.

And the economics underneath are diverging fast. MLB looks more and more like global entertainment: a champion built like a multinational, broadcasts sliced for international windows, stars functioning as markets. The KBO stays proudly, profitably domestic. Neither model is wrong. But they're pulling the sport's center of gravity in opposite directions, and the players feel the pull most. Every Korean star now grows up with two careers in his head, the beloved-at-home one and the tested-abroad one. And the league that raised him has to plan for the day he leaves.

**The Sofa Knows**

The analytics movement taught us to distrust narrative. Fair. Most of what feels like fate is variance wearing a costume. But thirty-six hours of finals baseball kept showing me things no model measures. The sound a stadium makes when belief drains out of it. The sound another one makes when a thirty-seven-year-old rounds first with the series in his pocket. That particular Game 7 hush between pitches, forty thousand people simultaneously unable to breathe.

Baseball stages these moments in any language. This autumn I needed two of them.

I regret nothing except the sleep.
