Last weekend delivered the rarest gift in a baseball fan's calendar: two championships, two continents, one exhausted sofa. On Friday night in Daejeon, the LG Twins finished off the Hanwha Eagles to win the Korean Series. Barely a day later, in Toronto, the Los Angeles Dodgers won one of the great Game 7s ever played, going eleven innings to beat the Blue Jays and become baseball's first repeat champion in a quarter century. I watched both, in two languages, and I have been thinking since about what each finale revealed about the sport that staged it.

**The Game 7 That Had Everything**

Start in Toronto, because Game 7s like this come along once a decade. The Blue Jays, carrying a country's hopes deep into a November night, took a lead into the ninth inning with the title on the table. Then Miguel Rojas — the Dodgers' least likely hero, a veteran glove-first infielder — homered to tie it. In the eleventh, catcher Will Smith hit the shot that won it all, 5–4. And in between, the defending champions emptied the tank in a way that will be retold for years: Yoshinobu Yamamoto, who had already thrown a complete game earlier in the series, came out of the bullpen on no rest to close out the final innings. He was named World Series MVP with three wins — Games 2, 6, and 7 — a workload from another era, delivered by a pitcher in his second big-league season out of Japan.

At our roundtable last week, I predicted the series would go the distance because Toronto had "a building that shakes." It did shake. It just fell silent one swing too early. My colleague Anthony predicted the Dodgers because "depth beats destiny over seven games." I hate it when he's right, and he was precisely right: the difference in the end was that Los Angeles had one more trustworthy arm, one more professional at-bat, one more answer. That is what all that payroll actually purchases — not certainty, but answers, stacked deep enough to survive a night when destiny is swinging hard the other way.

**Meanwhile, in Daejeon**

Half a world away, the Korean Series told a different story in the same sport. The LG Twins beat the Hanwha Eagles in five games, and the series turned on a collapse-and-response worthy of any classic: after surrendering a six-run eighth inning to lose Game 3, LG answered in Game 4 with a six-run ninth of their own to steal it back — the kind of gut-punch exchange that decides short series. The Twins' fourth title arrived through the hands of series MVP Kim Hyun-soo, a thirty-seven-year-old veteran collecting his rings in the twilight of a great career.

If you have never watched KBO baseball, understand that the difference from MLB is not primarily talent; it is texture. Korean baseball is louder, more communal, more theatrical — cheering squads, drums, every hitter with his own song sung by thousands who never sit down. October in Jamsil or Daejeon feels less like attending a game than joining one. Where American baseball sells you tension in reverent silence, Korean baseball refuses to let you experience a single pitch alone.

**One Sport, Two Theories of Itself**

Here is what struck me, toggling between feeds. MLB's finale was a showcase of concentration: the best players from everywhere — Japan, Venezuela, Korea, everywhere — gathered onto a few super-rosters, playing for a global audience at one in the morning. The KBO's finale was a showcase of location: a league that belongs utterly to its place, its rhythms tuned to its own fans, its heroes household names at home and rumors abroad.

The economics beneath the two models are diverging fast. MLB increasingly resembles global entertainment — its champion built like a multinational, its broadcasts sliced for international windows, its stars functioning as markets. The KBO remains, proudly and profitably, a domestic institution. Neither model is wrong. But they are pulling the sport's center of gravity in opposite directions, and the players feel the pull most: every Korean star now grows up with two possible careers in his head, the beloved-at-home one and the tested-abroad one, and the league that trained him must plan around the day he leaves.

**What the Sofa Knows**

The analytics movement taught us to distrust narrative, and rightly so — most of what feels like fate is variance wearing a costume. But sitting through thirty-six hours of finals baseball, I kept noticing what no model measures: the sound a stadium makes when belief drains out of it, the sound another makes when a thirty-seven-year-old rounds first with the series in his pocket, the particular quiet of a Game 7 crowd between pitches, forty thousand people simultaneously unable to breathe.

Baseball's genius is that it stages these moments in any language. This autumn I needed two of them, and I regret nothing except the sleep.
