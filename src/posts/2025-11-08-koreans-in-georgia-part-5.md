The singing starts before the cars stop arriving. On a gray Saturday morning in November, in a borrowed wing of a Korean church outside Atlanta, the five- and six-year-olds are already tracing letters and singing the vowel songs, their voices drifting down the hallway the way a good smell drifts out of a kitchen, and about sixty children file in around them carrying the same backpacks they carried to school all week. Most of their classmates are still asleep somewhere, or already at travel soccer. The walls here have other plans for the morning: the fourteen consonants and ten vowels of hangul in bright laminated rows, and above the rows a map of a peninsula most of these children have visited only in summers, if at all.

This is Korean school — hangul hakgyo — and versions of it meet every Saturday morning across Georgia, in churches and community centers and rented classrooms. Anyone who has attended a weekend heritage school in any language would recognize the room. For the fifth part of my series I wanted to sit inside one and find out what actually happens there. I expected a language class. What I found was something closer to a negotiation between generations, conducted one worksheet at a time, and the whole negotiation is legible in the seating. The youngest children are delighted to be there, the way small children are delighted to be anywhere with songs in it. The middle grades are a mixed picture, some engaged, some enduring. And the teenagers, the handful who remain by middle school, sit in the back wearing the expression of people who have lost this negotiation with their parents and know it.

"I used to hate it," a ninth grader told me during the break between periods, without much drama, the way you might mention an old scar. "Every Saturday, when my other friends were hanging out. I used to ask my mom, why do I have to do this? I already speak English. We live here." She paused, and I let the pause sit there between us. "I don't hate it anymore. But I couldn't tell you exactly when that changed."

Her teacher has taught at the school for over a decade, long enough to narrate that arc before it happens, the way my mother knows the rice is done without lifting the lid. "The little ones come happily. The teenagers come angrily. And then, around sixteen or seventeen, something turns," she said. "They start asking different questions. Not 'why do I have to learn this,' but 'why didn't I learn more.' I tell them: that is why we kept the door open. So that when you were ready to walk through it, it would still be there."

Sitting in on the classes, I kept noticing how little of the curriculum is really about grammar. The younger children learn the language of family: the honorifics for grandmother and grandfather, the correct way to address an older cousin, the verbs of the dinner table. The older ones read folk tales and write short letters to their parents in Korean, an assignment the teacher admits is partly a covert operation. "Some things," she said, "are easier to say in the language your mother dreams in."

Underneath every one of these Saturday mornings sits the question of whether a family will remain fully able to talk to itself. Many of the students' parents speak English well but feel in Korean. Many grandparents speak little English at all. The children think in English, joke in English, argue in English. Linguists call the gap "receptive bilingualism," understanding a language without comfortably speaking it, and in immigrant families it can narrow the channel between the generations down to logistics: eat, sleep, homework, hurry. A whole kitchen table's worth of love, reduced to the grocery list. Korean school is the community's organized resistance to that narrowing, and it is imperfect resistance, and everyone involved knows it; three hours a week cannot outtalk a hundred waking hours of English. The realistic goal, one parent volunteer told me, is not fluency. "It's that when she calls her grandmother, there is something there. A bridge. Even a narrow bridge is a bridge."

There is something else happening in those classrooms too, harder to put on a syllabus. For many of these children, weekdays are spent being the only Korean student in the room, or one of a few, fielding the familiar questions, explaining the food in their lunchbox, being gently exotic. On Saturday, for three hours, being Korean is not a distinguishing feature. It is the baseline, unremarkable and unexamined, comfortable as a house where you are not a guest. Several students, independently, described the feeling in almost the same words: "I don't have to explain anything here."

I thought about that on the drive home, the pines going by in the gray. In this series I have written about restaurants and churches, about a war veteran and a divided sanctuary, and the whole time I have been circling one question: how a community keeps itself whole while becoming something new. The Saturday school might be the most honest answer I have found, because it is so unsentimental about its own odds. Nobody there believes these children will grow up Korean the way their parents did. The project is smaller and braver than that — to make sure that whatever these children do grow up to be, the language of their grandmothers remains open to them, a door kept deliberately, stubbornly unlocked.

The teenagers in the back row will roll their eyes for a few more years yet. The teachers will keep showing up anyway, keeping the lights on and the vowel songs going and the door oiled on its hinges. Patience like that, it seems to me, is its own kind of love letter, written one Saturday at a time, sixty backpacks deep.
