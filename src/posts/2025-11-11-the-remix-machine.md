For a few weeks this fall, the most popular app in America was a machine for making culture out of other people's culture.

OpenAI's Sora, a social app wrapped around an AI video generator, rocketed to the top of the App Store within a day of launching. Its feed instantly filled with short, uncanny, often genuinely funny clips: familiar cartoon characters in impossible situations, dead celebrities delivering monologues they never gave, franchises colliding like action figures in a child's hands. It was mesmerizing. It was also, if you happened to own any of those characters or faces, industrial-scale unauthorized use, and the launch policy said the quiet part in the settings menu: your creations are in the machine unless you ask for them to be taken out.

Opt-out, not opt-in. Which is to say: a century of copyright practice, inverted by a toggle.

**The Fastest Backpedal in Tech**

What happened next happened fast. The Motion Picture Association — Hollywood's collective voice — publicly demanded that OpenAI stop the infringement, pointedly noting that the burden of protecting rights had been dumped onto the rights holders themselves. Within days, OpenAI reversed course: characters would now require opt-in permission, and rights holders would get finer controls. By the end of the month the fight had gone international, with a coalition of eighteen major Japanese publishers and studios, guardians of the anime and manga canon that Sora users had gleefully strip-mined, jointly accusing the company of infringement.

The speed of the reversal is the tell. This was not an accident discovered and corrected; the launch policy was a choice, made by a company sophisticated enough to know exactly what its feed would fill with. The strategy has a name in tech circles, ship first and negotiate later, and it has a trophy case: entire industries, from music streaming to user-video platforms, were built by companies that treated permission as a follow-up task. The Sora episode tested whether the playbook still works when the machine doesn't just *host* culture but *generates* it. The answer, at least this round: not quite. The backlash arrived in days, not years, because everyone has seen this movie before, and this time the movie was literally made of movies.

**Not Your Usual Copyright Brawl**

It's tempting to file this under "copyright dispute," a genre as old as the printing press. But something structurally new is happening here, and it deserves a precise name.

Copyright fights used to be about copies: who may reproduce a finished work. The AI fights are about capability. A model trained on decades of culture doesn't hold copies of it so much as it absorbs the style, the physics, the vibe, and can then produce infinite new almost-instances on demand. When anyone can generate a convincing new scene starring a studio's mascot, the studio hasn't lost one work. It has lost exclusivity over the ability to make more — which is the actual asset. The character stops being a product and becomes a genre anyone can write in.

And it isn't only corporations with mascots. The same machinery generates convincing video of real people, which is why, amid the cartoon chaos, the estates of deceased performers and the families of public figures found themselves playing whack-a-mole with digital resurrections. The question "who owns a fictional plumber" is commercially enormous but philosophically easy. The question "who owns your face after you're gone" is neither.

**In Praise of the Problem, Sort Of**

Because I write about culture for a living (a living measured in bylines, but still), I'm obligated to resist the purely defensive framing and say the complicated part out loud: the feed was often *wonderful*. Parody, mashup, and remix are not parasites on culture. They *are* culture, and always have been; fan fiction, sampling, and meme-making are how living audiences metabolize the stories they love. A tool that hands every teenager a film studio is, in the abstract, the most democratizing creative technology since the camera phone. Some of what filled Sora's feed was exactly the folk creativity that copyright's fair-use tradition exists to protect. I say this as someone whose entire job is remixing other people's work into columns, so the bias is disclosed.

The problem was never that people were playing with culture. The problem was the business model underneath the playground: a private company monetizing the play, at planetary scale, using creative labor it neither paid for nor asked about, with the costs of objection billed to the creators. Remix culture and permission culture have always negotiated their border case by case. Sora tried to move the border unilaterally and charge admission at the new gate.

**Coming Soon to a Courtroom Near You**

The next few years settle what this episode only posed. Whether training on copyrighted work requires a license: courts and legislatures in several countries are circling it. Whether "opt out" survives as a legal fiction anywhere. Whether new licensing markets emerge, where studios rent their universes to AI platforms the way they license theme parks, a future both sides are busily building toward even while suing each other. And whether individual creators, who have neither the MPA's lawyers nor the Japanese publishers' unity, get any seat at the table at all.

What the Sora weeks made vivid is that these stopped being abstract policy debates. The remix machine exists, it is delightful, it is voracious, and it is pointed directly at everything anyone has ever made. Culture has survived every previous copying technology and usually come out richer. But it never got richer by accident. Each round took decades of fighting over one deceptively simple question, the same one your kindergarten teacher asked before anybody touched the crayons: did you ask first?
