When people learn that I am Korean but have lived in Abu Dhabi for twelve years, they usually ask one of two questions. The first is whether I speak Arabic (some, and less than I should). The second, from Korean relatives especially, is more revealing: "But where will you really be from?"

I have stopped trying to answer that question and started noticing what it assumes—that a person must be from exactly one place, formed by exactly one system. My childhood has been an experiment in the alternative. And because so much of any childhood happens at school, what I have really been given is a twelve-year comparative education study, conducted from the inside.

Abu Dhabi is one of the most internationalized education markets on Earth. The emirate hosts schools teaching British, American, Indian, French, German, Japanese, and IB curricula, side by side, sometimes on the same street. My own schooling has been a relay race between systems. My classmates have held passports from thirty countries. Nobody was the default. Everyone was translating.

Here is what that vantage point teaches you.

First: every school system is a theory of what a person should be. You cannot see this from inside one system, any more than a fish sees water. The British curriculum I encountered prizes early specialization—by sixteen, students narrow to a few subjects, on the theory that depth forms intellect. The American approach keeps everything open until the last possible moment, on the theory that breadth forms character. Korean education, which reached me through hagwon-style academies, weekend classes, and my parents' expectations, treats mastery through repetition as a moral discipline, almost a form of respect. None of these is neutral. Each one quietly installs a different answer to the question: what is a mind for?

Second: students absorb the theory, not just the content. My friends from different systems don't just know different things; they are confident in different ways. The ones trained in British classrooms write essays that argue. The ones from American classrooms speak up first and revise later. My Korean-schooled cousins solve problems with a stamina that startles my classmates. I used to think one of these must be the correct product of education. Now I think each system simply manufactures the excellence it decided in advance to measure.

Third—and this is the part I find hopeful—children translate better than institutions do. On paper, my school years look like bureaucratic chaos: mismatched grade levels, curricula that disagree about when you learn algebra, three different narratives of world history in which the same events play different roles. In practice, the translation itself became the education. When you have heard the history of the twentieth century taught three ways, you do not conclude that history is unknowable. You conclude that every telling has an author. That single realization—every telling has an author—may be worth more than any individual syllabus I completed.

I don't want to romanticize this. A childhood between systems has real costs, and I know classmates who paid them: the ones who moved too often to keep friends, who are fluent in four languages and at home in none, who freeze when adults ask that innocent question about where they're from. Rootlessness is not a curriculum. The researchers who study "third culture kids" note both halves honestly—unusual adaptability and perspective on one side; a lingering sense of belonging everywhere and nowhere on the other. Both halves are true. I carry both.

But here is why I think this matters beyond people with unusual passports. The condition I was raised in—multiple systems, no default, constant translation—is no longer exotic. It is roughly the condition of being young now, anywhere. A teenager in Seoul or Statesboro toggles daily between the value systems of their family, their school, and a global internet that answers to neither. Every young person is now educated by competing systems that disagree about what a mind is for. In that sense, we are all in the international school. Some of us just got there earlier.

So when I write about education in these pages—and I intend to—it will be from this angle: not which system is best, but what each system is silently teaching, and what happens to the students asked to live between them. The place I am "really from" turns out to be that in-between space. It is more crowded than you would think. And it has a better view.
