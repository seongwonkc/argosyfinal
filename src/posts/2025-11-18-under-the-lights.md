There are two moments every week when my town gathers in one place. The first is Friday night, under stadium lights you can see from the highway, when several thousand people assemble to watch seventeen-year-olds play football. The second is Sunday morning, across a couple dozen churches, when many of the same people assemble again. I have spent years moving between these two gatherings, and my first piece for The Argosy is about what I found there: not two separate worlds, but one continuous one.

Start with the numbers, because outsiders never believe them. High school football stadiums in Texas can seat more people than some college venues; a town of ten thousand can put a meaningful fraction of itself in the bleachers on a Friday. The marching band has rehearsed all week. The dance team has rehearsed all week. Little kids play their own imaginary games behind the stands, wearing jerseys with the names of teenagers they idolize. The concession stand is run by somebody's mother, the chain gang by somebody's grandfather. It took me years to understand that the football is almost beside the point. Friday night is the town performing itself, to itself—a weekly ritual of belonging with a scoreboard attached.

If that sounds like a description of church, it should. The parallels are not subtle. Both have liturgy: the national anthem, the fight song, the alma mater sung win or lose, arms around shoulders. Both have vestments and processions, sacred spaces you do not walk on, elders who guard tradition. Both mark the calendar—the season, with its ordinary weeks and its high holy days against the rival town. And both promise the same big thing: that you, individually small, are part of a body that is larger and older than you and will outlast you.

The connection is more than structural. Here, faith and football are openly intertwined in ways that would startle people from elsewhere. Coaches speak the language of ministry—shepherding, sacrifice, being "poured into." Churches schedule around game nights and host team dinners. Players gather at the fifty-yard line after the final whistle, both teams together, to pray—kids who spent two hours hitting each other kneeling in one circle. The first time I saw that, still new to all of it, I remember thinking: whatever is happening here, it is not just sports.

I come at this as an observer with one foot inside. My family's faith came with us to Texas, the way faith often travels with immigrants—as an anchor when everything else moves. Church was where my parents found people who would explain the school system, recommend a mechanic, celebrate our small victories. So I understood viscerally, and early, what a congregation is for. What Friday night taught me is that a town can have more than one kind.

But honest observation means seeing the shadows too, and there are shadows. A ritual that binds a community together defines who belongs to it, and the definition is not always generous. The intensity poured into teenage athletes can curdle into pressure no seventeen-year-old should carry: playing hurt, being a town's emotional weather system, hearing grown men discuss your failures at the gas station. The same overlap of faith and football that moves me can exclude the classmate who worships differently, or not at all, and still just wants to play. And every year, some boys learn too late that the town's love was for the jersey, not the kid wearing it. A community's most beautiful rituals and its bluntest ones are usually the same rituals. That is worth saying plainly, and I intend to keep saying it in this column.

Still, I keep going back, and not only for the games. In an era when we are all supposedly atomized—alone with our screens, sorted into our private algorithmic worlds—I get to watch, twice a week, the stubborn persistence of physical gathering. Nobody streams Friday night alone in their room. You have to be there, in the cold, next to people you did not choose, singing words you did not write. Sociologists have a term for the emotion this produces—collective effervescence, the electric feeling of being one body—and they note, with some alarm, how few places still reliably generate it. My town has at least two. They meet on Friday and on Sunday, and they are, I have come to believe, the same congregation in different clothes.

This column will live at that intersection—where sports meets faith, community, and identity, in Texas and beyond. There is more happening under those lights than a game. Come stand in the bleachers with me and I'll show you.
