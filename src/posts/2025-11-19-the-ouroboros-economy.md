There is an ancient symbol called the ouroboros: a serpent devouring its own tail, forming a perfect circle. It represents eternity, self-renewal, and — depending on your reading — a creature that has mistaken itself for a meal. I can think of no better logo for the financial architecture of the artificial intelligence boom, which this month finally prompted the market to ask the impolite question: how much of this circle is real?

**The Deals That Draw a Circle**

Follow the announcements of the past two months as a diagram, and watch the arrows bend.

In September, the world's dominant AI chipmaker announced its intention to invest up to $100 billion in the world's most famous AI lab — money that the lab would then spend, in large part, deploying gigawatts of systems built on that same chipmaker's hardware. Investor and vendor, on both ends of one transaction. In October, the lab struck a massive chip-supply deal with a rival semiconductor firm that came bundled with warrants for roughly a tenth of that firm's stock — vesting as the chips deploy — meaning the customer earns equity in its supplier by shopping there, and the supplier's share price leaps on news of a sale partly paid for in its own shares. Days later, another AI-infrastructure titan announced a staggering backlog of future contract revenue, an enormous share of it attributable to a single customer: the same lab, again, whose ability to pay depends substantially on capital raised from the companies it is buying from.

Each deal, examined alone, has a defensible logic: suppliers locking in their most important customer, a lab locking in scarce computing power years ahead. Examined together, they form something stranger — a closed loop in which a handful of companies invest in each other, buy from each other, and book each other's promises as growth, while their combined market value rises on the strength of the loop itself. Money leaves one pocket, circles the table, and returns as everyone's revenue.

**Why This Time Rhymes**

Students of financial history will feel a chill of recognition, because we have seen this geometry before. In the late-1990s telecom bubble, equipment makers lent money to their own customers to buy their own gear — "vendor financing" — and booked the sales as if the demand were organic. When the customers failed, the vendors discovered they had been their own biggest buyers all along. The dot-com era added "round-tripping": companies swapping capacity with each other and recording both directions as revenue.

The AI loop is not identical — crucially, today's central players are enormously profitable, and the computing demand is real, measurable, and growing. But the structural rhyme matters. Circular financing does one dangerous thing regardless of era: it makes demand unfalsifiable from the outside. When the customer's money comes from the supplier, a sale stops being independent evidence that anyone outside the circle wants the product at the price charged. The numbers can all be accurate and still describe a hall of mirrors.

There is a second rhyme worth noting: the headline figures are softer than they look. The famous $100 billion investment is, as of this writing, a letter of intent — a memorandum of enthusiasm, not a signed check. Backlogs are promises about the future recorded in the present. Warrants vest on milestones that may not arrive. The boom's arithmetic is built substantially on announcements, and announcements are the cheapest commodity in finance.

**November's Flinch**

Which brings us to this month. After a euphoric autumn — the chipmaker crossing five trillion dollars in market value, records falling weekly — the AI trade abruptly reversed. The tech-heavy indexes suffered their worst stretch since spring; the highest-flying AI names fell hardest; a famous investor who once starred in a movie about shorting the housing bubble disclosed bets against the sector's darlings. Nothing failed. No deal collapsed. Earnings were, in fact, excellent. The selloff was something subtler: the market pausing mid-euphoria to ask what fraction of the future it had already paid for, and whether the circle's revenues would ever have to be validated by boring, external customers — companies and consumers paying full price with their own money.

That validation question is the entire game. The world's businesses may well absorb this much computing power profitably; the technology is genuinely transformative, and betting against it outright has been a losing trade for years. But "transformative technology" and "correctly priced circle" are different claims. The railroads transformed America and still bankrupted a generation of their financiers. The internet changed everything and vaporized trillions on the way. Infrastructure booms have a habit of being right about the future and wrong about the invoice.

**How to Watch the Snake**

For readers who want to follow this story like an analyst rather than a fan, ignore the announcements and watch three quieter dials. First, external revenue: how much AI spending comes from customers outside the loop — enterprises, consumers — versus members of the circle funding each other. Second, conversion: how many letters of intent, backlogs, and frameworks turn into signed, cash-generating contracts. Third, financing sources: the boom increasingly borrows — through debt markets and private credit — to build data centers; debt, unlike enthusiasm, has a maturity date.

The ouroboros is a hypnotic creature. It is also, biologically speaking, not a sustainable diet. The AI economy will eventually have to eat something other than itself. The bull case is that it will — that external demand arrives in time, at scale, at price. The bear case is simply the diagram, read literally. This month, for the first time, the market held the diagram up to the light. It did not like everything it saw.
