Jamaica has been keeping weather records for a long time, through a century and a half of hurricanes. Nothing in those records prepared it for the end of this October, when Hurricane Melissa came ashore as a Category 5 with winds around 185 miles per hour — the strongest landfall the island has ever measured, and at its peak, tied among the mightiest Atlantic storms ever observed. The damage estimates that followed made it the costliest disaster in the country's history. Across the Caribbean, dozens died, most of them in Haiti, where the margin between heavy rain and mass casualty is thinnest.

One week later and half a world away, Typhoon Kalmaegi tore through the central Philippines and gave Cebu its worst flash flooding on record — over a hundred dead, hundreds of thousands displaced, a state of emergency, and the particular grief of rescuers dying in a helicopter crash on their way to help. Kalmaegi wasn't even finished; Vietnam was next.

Then, before the month was out, the Bay of Bengal and the Strait of Malacca produced back-to-back systems — one of them the first named cyclone observed in that strait in more than a century — whose rains sat over Sumatra, Sri Lanka, and southern Thailand until more than a thousand people were dead and half a million displaced across four countries.

Three regions. Six weeks. In each place, the same sentence in the news reports, translated into three languages: nothing like this in living memory.

**The Vocabulary Problem**

Here is the uncomfortable technical detail underneath that sentence. Our entire system for living with disasters — building codes, flood maps, insurance prices, evacuation plans — is built on a statistical idea: the return period. The "hundred-year flood" is shorthand for a flood with a one percent chance in any given year, estimated from the past century's records. Everything about how we build assumes the past is a decent preview of the future.

A warming climate quietly breaks that assumption. Warmer oceans feed storms more energy, so the strongest storms get stronger and intensify faster — sometimes exploding from ordinary to catastrophic in a day, faster than evacuation timelines were designed for. Warmer air holds more moisture — roughly seven percent more per degree Celsius — so the same storm now wrings out more rain over the same hillside. The dice haven't just been rolled badly this autumn; they are being reweighted mid-game. When a "once-a-century" event arrives twice in a decade, the phrase isn't unlucky. It's obsolete.

**What the Six Weeks Actually Teach**

It would be easy to leave this as a lament. But look closer at the wreckage and specific, fixable failures come into focus.

The first lesson is that the deadliest variable isn't wind speed — it's what the storm finds when it arrives. Melissa at Category 5 killed dozens in Jamaica; far weaker rainfall killed comparably in Haiti, where deforested hillsides and fragile settlements convert rain directly into mortality. Cebu's flooding was worsened by rivers that development had narrowed and clogged. The storm is weather; the disaster is infrastructure. That distinction sounds academic until you notice it's the difference between grief and shrug for identical rainfall totals.

The second lesson is that early warning is the cheapest life-saving technology we have, and it remains unevenly distributed. Forecasts for these storms were, by historical standards, remarkably good — the science saw them coming days out. The gap is the last mile: whether the warning reaches a fishing village, in the right language, with instructions people trust enough to act on, and whether there's anywhere safe to go. Closing that gap costs almost nothing next to the losses it prevents. The United Nations has a program aiming to get warning coverage to everyone on Earth; autumn 2025 is its strongest argument to date.

The third lesson is about money, and it's the one I suspect this column will return to for years. Insurance is how modern societies metabolize disaster — spreading one town's catastrophe across millions of premiums. But insurers price on those same broken return periods, and where the math stops working, they leave. Parts of the world — including wealthy corners of it — are becoming quietly uninsurable, which pushes the cost of catastrophe back onto whoever is standing there when it hits: usually governments, and ultimately the people least able to carry it. Meanwhile the countries drowning this autumn contributed among the least to the emissions loading the dice. That imbalance has a name in climate diplomacy — loss and damage — and a new fund with, so far, more sympathy than money in it.

**Against the Numbness**

I'm aware of the risk in writing this kind of piece: disaster lists numb people. Another storm, another toll, another appeal. So let me end somewhere specific instead.

In the reporting from Cebu, there's a detail I can't put down: neighbors forming human chains in chest-deep water, passing children toward higher ground — improvising, in the absence of adequate infrastructure, an infrastructure of their own bodies. Human beings are astonishing in the hour of catastrophe. The entire project of climate resilience is, in a sense, an attempt to be that good before the water arrives: to encode that solidarity into concrete, drainage, warning systems, insurance pools, and building codes — the boring, load-bearing forms of caring about strangers.

The atmosphere has changed its statistics. The question this autumn poses is whether we change ours — or keep pricing the future off a century that no longer exists.
