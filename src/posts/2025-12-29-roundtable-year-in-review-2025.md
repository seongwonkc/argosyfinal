*Editor's Note: Our year-in-review roundtable is a tradition, and it comes with two house rules: each editor must nominate one story as the year's headline, and each must confess one thing they got wrong. The transcript has been lightly edited for length and clarity.*

**Minwoo Jung:** Welcome to our last conversation of 2025 — and, I'll note with some feeling, the last year-in-review this particular group will hold together. House rules apply: one headline, one confession. I'll open with my headline: the Gaza ceasefire. In October, a war that defined two years of global politics stopped — hostages home, guns quiet, everything hard deferred but stopped. I wrote at the time that it was a pause with paperwork. The paperwork is holding better than I feared, which brings me to my confession: I underestimated exhaustion as a strategic force. Analysts model interests and capabilities. The variable that actually ended the war was that everyone, everywhere, could not bear it anymore.

**Anthony Min:** My headline is the AI economy going fully circular. The chipmaker investing in the lab that buys its chips, records falling weekly, five trillion dollars of market value in a single company, and then November's shudder when the market finally asked what's real. We will spend years finding out whether 2025 was 1996 or 1999. My confession: the government shutdown. In October I wrote a measured explainer assuming the usual script — a few weeks of theater, then a deal. It became the longest in American history, forty-three days, with the national debt crossing thirty-eight trillion dollars mid-blackout. I keep a note above my desk now: "Dysfunction can always exceed your model of it."

**Yonghyuk Choi:** Headline: the Dodgers going back-to-back in an eleven-inning Game 7 that two countries will never forget — with honorable mention to my beloved chaos of the same weekend, LG winning the Korean Series. Sport gave 2025 its best television and its worst behavior; Bethpage gave us both in one week. Confession: at our October roundtable I picked Toronto. On this masthead we honor our failed predictions publicly, like adults.

**Saerom Kim:** Mine is quieter. The year's headline, from where I sit in Georgia, was the ordinary persistence of communities under strain — the Korean churches, the Saturday schools, the small towns I've spent the year documenting. Nothing about that trends. Everything about it holds the world together while the headlines happen. My confession: when I started this series I quietly believed I was writing about other people. Somewhere around Part 5, I understood I've been writing my own application for belonging. The interviewer was always in the story.

**Yehee Jung:** Headline: the quiet dismantling of American vaccine architecture — panels remade, guidance rolled back, and by November, Canada losing its measles elimination status and the Americas losing theirs, a status it took generations to earn. It's the rare story that's slow, technical, and enormous. Confession: I spent the year writing about medicine's miracles — gene therapies, million-dollar cures — and I underweighted the unglamorous half of public health: trust. A therapy can be engineered in a decade. Trust, it turns out, can be un-engineered faster.

**Ethan Yang:** My headline is a split-screen. On one side: COP30 in the Amazon, a real forest fund launched, and clean energy breaking every deployment record on Earth. On the other: a Category 5 stronger than anything Jamaica ever measured, and a thousand dead in one month of Asian floods. Both sides are the true story of 2025; anyone who shows you only one is selling something. Confession: I began the year believing the AI boom would be mostly good news for the grid — demand that finances the clean build-out. The cornfields taught me it's messier. I still hold the optimist's position, but I hold it with both hands now.

**Minwoo Jung:** Before we close — someone should say it about the culture, since our culture desk is off this week. A pop star sold four million albums in seven days like it was 1999, the most anticipated video game on Earth got delayed again to next November, and an animated sequel about a rabbit police officer became the biggest cartoon in history mostly because of China. The monoculture our new colleague Patrick declared dead in October spent December staging selective resurrections. Culture refuses to obey its obituaries.

**Yonghyuk Choi:** And Nepal, Minwoo. Don't let the ceasefire hog your beat's headline — a government fell to a group chat this year.

**Minwoo Jung:** Duly noted, and watch that story: the election is in March. Which is a fitting place to end — 2026 arrives with its calendar already crowded. A Winter Olympics, a World Cup on this continent, elections from Kathmandu to Budapest, and for several of us at this table, some rather consequential envelopes in the spring. See you all in January.

---
### Final Thoughts

**Minwoo Jung:** 2025 was the year exhaustion outperformed strategy — wars paused, governments fell, and the young stopped waiting politely.

**Anthony Min:** The year's economy ran on promises about 2027. At some point the invoices arrive; keep the receipts.

**Yonghyuk Choi:** A sport is healthiest when its best story and its best team are different things. Baseball, this year, had both — and they met in Game 7.

**Saerom Kim:** The news measures what changed. Most of what matters is what held.

**Yehee Jung:** Medicine's frontier advanced and its foundation cracked, in the same twelve months. Only one of those makes headlines.

**Ethan Yang:** The transition is winning and the weather is worsening, and 2025 refused to simplify that sentence. So should we.

*What was your headline of 2025? Tell us in the comments — and thank you, sincerely, for a year of reading.*
