If you wanted to change what vaccines a country's children receive, how would you do it? You would not ban anything — bans produce lawsuits and headlines. You would do something quieter. You would change who sits on a committee.

That is the story of American vaccine policy over the past year, and it is worth telling carefully — not as polemic, but as anatomy. Because the machinery involved is obscure, the changes are technical, and the consequences will be measured in years, in other countries as well as in the United States. I study public health; this is the most important story on my beat, and the hardest to see whole.

**The Machinery**

Start with how the system worked. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices — ACIP — is a panel of outside experts that recommends which vaccines Americans should receive and when. Its recommendations are not laws, but they are load-bearing: insurance coverage, school requirements, and the federal program that buys vaccines for about half of American children all key off its votes. For decades it operated in professional obscurity, staffed by immunologists, pediatricians, and epidemiologists arguing over evidence tables on livestreams almost nobody watched.

Last year, the committee's entire membership was dismissed and replaced by the new health leadership in Washington — leadership with a long public record of vaccine skepticism. The remade panel, several of whose members arrived with their own records of questioning vaccine safety, began voting. At its first full meeting in September, it rolled back the recommendation for a combination childhood shot, moved COVID vaccination from a routine recommendation to "individual decision-making," and opened debate on the hepatitis B birth dose — a pillar of newborn protection since the 1990s. One new member described the panel, candidly, as "rookies." Pediatric societies, watching decades of accumulated procedure compressed and reversed in a single season, responded with something close to alarm: some now publish their own competing schedules, an extraordinary fracture in a system whose entire value was that it spoke with one voice.

**Why a Committee Matters More Than a Law**

Here is the mechanism I most want readers to understand. Vaccination runs on defaults, not mandates. The overwhelming majority of parents do not research immunology; they do what the pediatrician offers, and the pediatrician offers what the schedule recommends, and insurers pay for what the schedule contains. Shift a vaccine from "recommended" to "your choice," and nothing is forbidden — but the default flips. Some clinics stop stocking it. Some insurers reconsider. Some parents, hearing officialdom shrug, shrug too. Coverage does not collapse; it erodes, a point or two a year, unevenly, invisibly.

And vaccination is one of the few technologies where a few points matter enormously, because of thresholds. Measles — the canary of immunization systems, the most contagious disease we routinely prevent — needs roughly 95 percent coverage to stay contained. Below that, outbreaks stop being freak events and become weather.

**The Canary, Already Singing**

Which brings me to the part of this story that is no longer hypothetical. The United States just recorded its worst measles year in over three decades — thousands of cases, with deaths, including children, in a disease many young doctors had never seen outside textbooks. Kindergarten vaccination coverage has slipped below the containment threshold. And in November, something happened that public-health historians will mark: Canada — whose coverage had been sliding for years — lost its official measles elimination status, and because regional status depends on every member, the Americas as a whole lost theirs. The first region on Earth to eliminate measles is no longer measles-free. Elimination took generations of work. Losing it took about a decade of drift.

I want to be precise about causality, because precision is the whole game in my field: America's committee drama did not cause Canada's outbreak, and coverage was slipping in many countries before any recent political change. The forces are larger — pandemic-era disruption, misinformation ecosystems, and a broad erosion of trust in institutions of every kind. The committee story matters because it is an accelerant applied exactly where the system was already weakest: the default-setting machinery that made vaccination the easy path for busy, trusting, ordinary families.

**What I Am Watching in 2026**

Three indicators, for readers who want to follow this like an epidemiologist rather than a partisan. First, kindergarten coverage rates, published annually, state by state — the single best leading indicator, now below threshold and drifting. Second, the fate of the hepatitis B birth dose and other postponed votes, because newborn protections are where recommendation changes convert most directly into preventable illness. Third, the international echo: American health agencies have long served as reference points for poorer countries' policies, and the question of who fills that role now — and with what standards — may matter more globally than anything decided domestically.

The deeper lesson of this year is one I keep relearning on this beat: public health's greatest achievements make themselves invisible. A disease you never see, a committee you never hear of, a default you never question — the system worked precisely because nobody had to think about it. Its dismantling is proceeding the same way. Nothing has been banned. There was only a meeting, and then another, and a schedule that quietly says "your choice" where it used to say "protect them."

The diseases, patient as ever, are taking the choice seriously.
