There is a boy from my region of Texas — I'll keep him nameless, because his story is everyone's story now — who signed with a university last year for more money than his father has ever made in a year of honest work. Nobody hid this. It wasn't a scandal in a newspaper; it was a press conference with balloons. The town was proud. The town was also, in the pews and the parking lots, quietly asking a question it couldn't quite phrase: what exactly did we just watch?

What we watched was the new normal. This academic year is the first full season of the revolution: under the House legal settlement, universities can now pay their athletes directly — up to roughly twenty million dollars per school per year in shared revenue, a figure set to climb for a decade. Add the name-image-likeness deals that have flowed since 2021, now policed by a new clearinghouse, and the transfer portal that lets players change schools nearly as freely as coaches always could, and the old world — the amateur "student-athlete," bound to his school, compensated in tuition and glory — is simply gone. It died in courtrooms, where it turned out that a multibillion-dollar entertainment industry built on unpaid teenage labor could not survive contact with antitrust law.

**The Case for the Revolution, From the Bleachers**

Let me say the just part plainly, because it is the biggest part. The old system was indefensible, and everyone who loved it knew. Coaches earned more than university presidents — often more than anyone on the state payroll — while the young men generating the value risked their knees and brains for a scholarship they could lose. Towns like mine filled hundred-thousand-seat stadiums; television networks paid billions; and the athletes, disproportionately Black and disproportionately from families with little, were told that payment would corrupt the purity of the enterprise. Purity, conveniently, was always billed to the poorest people in the building.

So when the checks finally arrived, they arrived as justice, late. The boy with the press conference bought his mother a car. Multiply that by thousands of families and you have the moral core of this story, and nothing in the rest of this column retracts it.

**What the Money Dissolves**

But I write about sports and community, and from that angle something else is visible — something the lawyers' victory, right as it was, never had to price.

College fandom was always built on a lovely fiction: that the nineteen-year-old in your colors was of your place — your school, your town, someone's roommate, staying four years, playing for the name on the front. The fiction was never fully true. But institutions run on beloved fictions, and this one organized real loyalty: alumni who wept at games, towns that treated a roster as kin.

The portal and the paycheck dissolve the fiction on an annual schedule. Rosters now turn over like the pros — except faster, because pro leagues at least have contracts. A star arrives, shines, and enters the portal by spring for a better offer; fans learn not to attach. Coaches recruit their own players every winter, re-selling the program to its existing roster. The question hanging over every locker room is the free market's question — what am I worth elsewhere? — which is a fine question for labor and a corrosive one for belonging. We are discovering, season by season, how much of the sport's meaning was stored in the parts that didn't move.

And the money tilts, as money does. The powerhouse conferences can fund twenty million; the smaller schools — the mid-majors that gave March its madness, the programs whose entire identity was overachieving — increasingly serve as unwilling farm systems, developing talent the giants then purchase. Competitive romance was never fair, but it used to be possible. The new structure is honest and stratified, like most honest structures.

**Faith, Football, and the Whole Person**

Here is the tension I sit with, from my particular seat. The communities I write about — church communities, football communities, often the same families — believe formation matters: that a young person is not only a market value, and that institutions owe the whole person something beyond the check. The old college-sports system abused that belief shamelessly, preaching formation while extracting labor. The new system doesn't preach at all; it pays, and lets the eighteen-year-old and his agent manage the formation question themselves.

I can't mourn the old hypocrisy. I'm not yet ready to celebrate the new candor. A nineteen-year-old with a seven-figure valuation, a one-year commitment, and a public market for his services carries a weight no bleacher critic has ever carried; some will bear it beautifully — many already do, sending money home, building futures — and some will be consumed by it, and the institutions cashing the television checks have accepted remarkably little responsibility for which way it goes. If schools are now employers in fact, then the duties of an employer — guidance, protection, honesty about the odds — are the least the arrangement owes. The settlement priced the labor. Nobody has yet priced the care.

On Friday nights here, the high-school lights still come on, and beneath them the next boys run routes while their towns watch. What's changed is what waits after graduation: not a fiction of amateur glory, but a market — fairer, colder, and utterly indifferent to the name on the front of the jersey. Justice arrived in college sports. Belonging is now a free agent, and every fan in America is learning what we'll pay to keep it.
