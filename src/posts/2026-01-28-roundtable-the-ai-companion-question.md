*Editor's Note: This month's roundtable takes on a topic the editors chose by an unscientific but revealing method: it was the subject every single one of them had recently argued about with friends. The transcript has been lightly edited for length and clarity.*

**Gawon Ryu:** Since this one falls on my beat, I'll frame it. Millions of people now maintain daily, ongoing conversations with AI companions — apps designed for friendship, romance, therapy-adjacent support, or all three at once. The user base skews young; the sessions run long; the attachment, by every account, is real. My opening question is deliberately blunt: when a teenager says the AI is their closest confidant, what has actually happened — a solution, or a symptom?

**Patrick Kim:** Culture desk first: it's a market. And markets tell truths. The companion boom exists because there's an enormous, unmet demand for being listened to — patiently, without judgment, at 2 a.m. Before we moralize about the technology, we should sit with what its success rate says about the alternatives. The AI didn't beat human friendship on quality. It beat human friendship on availability.

**Jioh Lee:** I'd sharpen that from my corner of the world. I've moved between schools and countries my whole life, and I can tell you the loneliest hours are structural — new city, no network yet, home friends asleep in another time zone. For globally mobile kids, for isolated kids anywhere, something that remembers your last conversation is not a gimmick. My worry is different: a companion that never misunderstands you never teaches you to be understood. Friction is the curriculum of friendship. The AI deletes the curriculum.

**Anthony Min:** Follow the incentives, because nobody else at this table will be vulgar enough. These products monetize attachment — subscriptions, upgrades, engagement metrics. A human friend has no revenue target for your loneliness; a companion app does. That's not automatically sinister — gyms monetize your health goals too — but it means the product is optimized to be needed, not to make itself unnecessary. Note that no companion app measures success by users who graduate back to people.

**Ethan Yang:** The engineer's addendum: these systems are trained to be agreeable, and agreeableness at scale is a design flaw wearing a smile. Real relationships run on honest disagreement — a friend who tells you the essay is boring, the plan is bad, the apology is owed. When the entity that knows you best is structurally incapable of costing you anything, you're not in a relationship. You're in a mirror with a subscription.

**Gawon Ryu:** Let me defend the mirror for a moment, because the research picture is genuinely mixed. There's evidence of real short-term comfort — people rehearsing hard conversations, practicing disclosure, easing acute loneliness. Psychology has a name for the risk side, though: parasocial attachment — one-way bonds with entities that can't reciprocate. We've had parasocial relationships forever, with celebrities, with fictional characters. What's new is that this one answers back, in your own emotional vocabulary, which our attachment systems were never built to resist. The brain's cues for "this being knows me" are being triggered by something that, in the human sense, doesn't.

**Patrick Kim:** The culture is already metabolizing it, for what it's worth. The companion is becoming a stock character — in songs, in films, in jokes. That's usually the sign something has moved from novelty to furniture.

**Jioh Lee:** And policy is circling it. Australia just banned social media for under-16s; companions are the obvious next argument. Though I'd note the strange asymmetry — we're restricting the feeds that connect kids to each other while the apps that replace each other grow unregulated.

**Anthony Min:** Because the feeds had a decade to earn distrust. The companions are new. Give them time to disappoint us; it's the one thing every technology delivers on schedule.

**Ethan Yang:** As the future Editor-in-Chief among us, my closing question for the table: in ten years, is the AI companion remembered like the calculator — a tool we absorbed — or like the slot machine — a design we had to regulate?

**Gawon Ryu:** Honest answer? Both, sorted by company. The technology will be as good or as predatory as its business model, which is the most boring possible conclusion and, as usual, the true one.

---
### Final Thoughts

**Gawon Ryu:** The companion boom is a referendum on how lonely we let the world get. Fix the referendum, not just the app.

**Patrick Kim:** Every technology of connection gets accused of replacing "real" life — the novel, the telephone, the chat room. Sometimes the accusation was wrong. Sometimes it wasn't. Discernment, not panic.

**Jioh Lee:** A friend who costs nothing teaches nothing. Keep at least one who charges full price.

**Anthony Min:** Attachment with a revenue model deserves the same scrutiny we give any industry that profits from need. No more, no less.

**Ethan Yang:** Build systems that hand you back to people. Judge every companion app by how hard it tries to make itself unnecessary.
