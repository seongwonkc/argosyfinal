*Editor's Note: This month's topic was chosen by an unscientific but revealing method: it was the subject every editor had recently argued about with friends. Lightly edited for length and clarity.*

**Gawon Ryu:** Since this one lands on my beat, I'll frame it. Millions of people now hold daily, ongoing conversations with AI companions — apps built for friendship, romance, something therapy-shaped, or all three at once. The users skew young. The sessions run long. The attachment, by every account I can find, is real.

**Gawon Ryu:** So, bluntly: when a teenager says the AI is their closest confidant — what has actually happened? A solution, or a symptom?

**Patrick Kim:** Culture desk goes first, apparently. Fine: it's a *market*. And markets tell the truth even when nobody wants it.

**Patrick Kim:** The companion boom exists because there's enormous unmet demand for being listened to. Patiently. Without judgment. At 2 a.m.

**Patrick Kim:** Which is to say: the AI didn't beat human friendship on quality. It beat human friendship on *availability*.

**Jioh Lee:** May I sharpen that from my corner of the world. I have moved between schools and countries my whole life, and the loneliest hours are structural ones. New city. No network yet. Home friends asleep in a different time zone.

**Jioh Lee:** For globally mobile kids — for isolated kids anywhere — something that remembers your last conversation is not a gimmick.

**Jioh Lee:** My worry is a different one. A companion that never misunderstands you never teaches you to be understood. Friction is the curriculum of friendship. The AI deletes the curriculum.

**Anthony Min:** Someone has to follow the incentives, so I will. These products monetize attachment. Subscriptions. Upgrades. Engagement metrics.

**Anthony Min:** A human friend has no revenue target for your loneliness. A companion app does. Not automatically sinister — gyms monetize your health goals — but the product is optimized to be needed. Not to make itself unnecessary.

**Anthony Min:** Notice that no companion app reports the number of users who graduated back to people. Ask yourself why that metric doesn't exist.

**Ethan Yang:** The engineer's addendum: these systems are trained toward agreeableness, and agreeableness at scale is a design flaw wearing a smile.

**Ethan Yang:** Real relationships run on honest disagreement. The friend who says the essay is boring. The one who says the apology is owed. When the entity that knows you best is structurally incapable of costing you anything — that's not a relationship. That's a mirror with a subscription.

**Gawon Ryu:** Let me defend the mirror for one minute, because the research picture genuinely is mixed. There's decent evidence of short-term comfort — people rehearsing hard conversations, practicing disclosure, easing acute loneliness. That's real and I won't pretend otherwise.

**Gawon Ryu:** The risk side has an old name, though: parasocial attachment. One-way bonds with entities that can't reciprocate. We've always had them — celebrities, fictional characters.

**Gawon Ryu:** What's new is that this one answers back, in your own emotional vocabulary. Our attachment system's cues for "this being knows me" are being triggered by something that, in the human sense, doesn't.

**Patrick Kim:** Meanwhile the culture has already metabolized it. The AI companion is a stock character now — in songs, in movies, in jokes. That's the tell that something has moved from novelty to furniture.

**Jioh Lee:** And policy is circling. Australia has just switched off social media for under-16s; the companions are the obvious next argument. Though observe the asymmetry — we restrict the feeds that connect children to each other, while the apps that replace each other grow unregulated.

**Anthony Min:** Because the feeds had a decade to earn distrust. The companions are new. Give them time. Disappointment is the one thing every technology ships on schedule.

**Ethan Yang:** Chair's closing question, then. Ten years from now: is the AI companion remembered like the calculator — a tool we absorbed — or like the slot machine — a design we had to regulate?

**Gawon Ryu:** Honest answer? Both, sorted by company. The technology will be exactly as decent or as predatory as its business model. (Which is the most boring possible conclusion, and, as usual on this beat, the true one.)

---
### Final Thoughts

**Gawon Ryu:** The companion boom is a referendum on how lonely we've let things get. Fix the referendum, not just the app.

**Patrick Kim:** Every connection technology gets accused of replacing "real" life — the novel, the telephone, the chat room. Sometimes the accusation was wrong. Sometimes it wasn't. Discernment over panic.

**Jioh Lee:** A friend who costs nothing teaches nothing. Keep at least one who charges full price.

**Anthony Min:** Attachment with a revenue model gets the same scrutiny as any industry that profits from need. No more. No less.

**Ethan Yang:** Judge every companion app by one spec: how hard it works to hand you back to people.
