Within an hour's drive of my home in Abu Dhabi, you can walk through the campus of a major New York research university, attend a lecture at a branch of the Sorbonne, and pass buildings flying the flags of universities from three continents. None of these institutions existed here thirty years ago. Most didn't exist twenty years ago. They were summoned — funded, built, and staffed at extraordinary speed — by governments that decided, with the directness only oil wealth allows, that if their countries needed world-class universities, they would simply buy them.

Having grown up inside this experiment, and having written in these pages about what a childhood between school systems teaches, I want to examine the boldest version of that in-betweenness: not a student moving between systems, but entire systems being transplanted, wholesale, across the world. The Gulf's imported campuses are the most interesting test anywhere of a deceptively simple question — is a university a thing you can ship?

**Why the Gulf Is Buying**

The motive is written in every regional planning document: the oil will not last forever, and everyone knows the date is closer than it used to be. The Gulf states are in a race to convert hydrocarbon wealth into post-hydrocarbon economies — finance, logistics, tourism, technology — and every version of that future requires educated people in quantities the region's own institutions could not historically produce. Importing established universities does three things at once: it trains the workforce, it signals seriousness to global investors, and it keeps at home the ambitious local students who would otherwise take their tuition and, often, their futures to London or Boston.

There is also a subtler motive, familiar to anyone from a small country living beside giants: prestige is protection. A city hosting the campuses, museums, and conferences of the world weaves itself into the world's interests. The university is soft-power infrastructure, as strategic in its way as a port.

**What Money Can Build**

Let me report honestly what the money has built, because caricature is easy from far away. The facilities are extraordinary — laboratories and libraries that faculty from the home campuses describe with open envy. The student bodies are among the most international on Earth; my own schooling, with its thirty passports in a classroom, was a preview of these universities, where a seminar might hold no two students from the same country. Serious research happens. Serious teaching happens. For a bright student from Amman, Lagos, Dhaka, or for that matter Seoul, the Gulf campuses offer something real: an elite education, closer to home, often generously funded.

And the experiment has already changed its hosts. A generation of Gulf women, in particular, has surged into higher education — in several countries they now outnumber men on campus — with consequences for family, work, and society that are still unfolding and, I suspect, unstoppable.

**What Money Cannot Ship**

But a university is not only classrooms and faculty. It is a set of habits — and the deepest habit of the great universities is inconvenient: they are machines for protected disagreement. Tenure, academic freedom, the right to research what power finds uncomfortable and publish what it finds embarrassing — these evolved over centuries, often in direct conflict with the states that funded them. That is the component that does not ship easily.

The Gulf campuses live inside states that are generous patrons and firm editors. Certain political topics are handled carefully or not at all; certain speakers do not get invited; faculty — and, in documented cases, students and researchers — have learned where the boundaries sit, sometimes at the airport. The home institutions, having taken the funding, absorb recurring controversies about what their branches may say. Defenders argue, not absurdly, that engagement moves boundaries more than boycott ever could, and that the campuses are among the freest rooms in their societies. Critics answer that a university that cannot ask every question is a different institution wearing the same crest. Having sat in those classrooms, I can report the truth is genuinely both: the rooms are freer than the country, and less free than the crest implies. Everyone inside knows the unwritten map.

**The Experiment's Real Question**

Here is what I think the Gulf experiment is actually testing, and why the world should watch it without smugness. The Western research university bundled several things together — training, credentialing, research, and dissent — and its history suggests the bundle mattered: the same protections that shelter the annoying philosopher shelter the inconvenient data. The Gulf model proposes an unbundling: import the training, the credentialing, and the research; regulate the dissent. If the unbundled version works — if it produces the engineers, the patents, the diversified economy — then a powerful lesson will be drawn far beyond the Gulf, in every capital that would love excellence without argument. If it stalls — if it turns out that the friction was the engine — that will be a different lesson, purchased at fabulous expense.

I have too much affection for this place, and too much respect for what has been built here in a single generation, to root against it. The city that raised me was itself an experiment nobody believed in. But I notice what I absorbed in those thirty-passport classrooms, the lesson no ministry planned: that every telling has an author, that systems can be compared, that questions travel even when speakers cannot. Education, once imported, does not stay inside its boundaries. That may prove the experiment's most important result — and the one nobody budgeted for.
