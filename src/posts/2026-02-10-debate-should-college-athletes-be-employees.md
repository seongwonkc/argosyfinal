*Editor's Note: Our Debate format pairs two writers on opposite sides of one question. With university revenue-sharing now a settled fact, the live question in American college sports is whether athletes should become what the law calls them nowhere and the economics calls them everywhere: employees. Sports editor Yonghyuk Choi argues yes; sports & society contributor Kyusung Lee argues no. Each wrote independently, then read the other's case before writing a short reply.*

---

## Yes: Call It What It Is — Yonghyuk Choi

The question sounds radical and is actually clerical. College athletes at the top level already work under conditions any labor lawyer would recognize instantly: schedules set by supervisors, physical risk as a job requirement, performance reviews with termination consequences, revenue generated in the billions, and now — after the settlement era began — direct payment for services. The only thing missing is the word. My case is that the word matters, and that withholding it is the last trick of a system that spent a century underpaying its workforce through vocabulary.

Consider what the current halfway house actually is. Schools pay athletes millions in "revenue share" while insisting they are not employees — which means no contracts with guaranteed terms, no collective bargaining, no workers' compensation regime built for their injuries, no seat at the table where the rules of their own industry are written. Every protection an employee would get arrives, if at all, as a gift from the same institutions whose interests run the other way. We have re-created the company town, with better graphics.

Employment fixes the architecture, not just the paycheck. Contracts would stabilize the very chaos traditionalists mourn: a signed three-year deal binds a player to a program far more effectively than amateur nostalgia ever did — ask any professional league, where rosters are more stable than the transfer portal, not less. Unions would negotiate the things no individual nineteen-year-old can: health coverage that outlasts eligibility, practice limits, a cut of media rights, standards for how schools handle injury and dismissal. And the bargaining table would finally include the people the entire enterprise is built on.

The standard objections dissolve on contact. "Most programs lose money" — most athletes in money-losing sports would fall outside employment tests anyway, which turn on revenue and control; nobody is proposing salaried rowers. "It will kill Olympic sports" — schools chose to fund those sports from football surpluses and can keep choosing to; a university that can pay a coach eight figures can keep the swim team. "They're students first" — so are the students paid to staff the library, the lab, and the dining hall, all employees, none corrupted by the W-2.

Kyusung will tell you, eloquently, that something communal dies when the jersey becomes a job. I answer: the something was already dead, killed not by the athletes but by the adults who monetized every square inch of the enterprise and exempted only the labor. You cannot sell a sport for billions and then ask its workers to preserve, unpaid, the amateur soul you sold. The soul left in the television deal. All that remains is to pay the people honestly — and honest payment, in this country, comes with a word.

---

## No: The Job Will Eat the School — Kyusung Lee

I concede Yonghyuk's history — the old system was exploitation with a fight song, and I've said so in these pages. What I contest is his remedy, because employment is not a payment mechanism. It is a relationship, with its own logic, and that logic devours everything adjacent to it.

Make the athlete an employee and the university becomes, in law and inevitably in practice, an employer. Employers manage assets. Underperforming assets are released — no more of the soft landing that lets a benched sophomore keep his scholarship and his degree; a contract cuts both ways, and the school's side will be written by the same athletic departments Yonghyuk rightly distrusts. Employees can be traded, assigned, restructured; the first conference-wide labor agreement will contain, within a decade, mechanisms no one now proposes aloud. And the student half of "student-athlete" — already thin — becomes a compliance fiction: why would an employer schedule around organic chemistry? The degree, the one asset that outlasts every knee, quietly exits the bargain. Ask the minor leagues how much education travels with a paycheck.

Then there's the map. Employment law is state law and vote-by-vote politics; the moment athletes are employees, fifty legislatures and every public-university board become sports regulators. States will compete on labor terms the way they compete on taxes; the schism between the dozen programs that can run payrolls and the hundreds that cannot stops being a gap and becomes a legal wall. The March tournament that lets a small school shock the world exists because everyone is, formally, playing the same game. Employment ends the formality — and with it, eventually, the game.

My deeper objection, though, comes from the bleachers I write from. The claim isn't that amateurism was pure — it's that the college game's value, including its market value, flows from its being embedded in something that isn't a firm: a campus, a town, a student section that believes the linebacker sits two rows over in history class. Embeddedness is why a college rivalry outdraws professional teams in the same state. Convert the enterprise into employer-and-staff, and you don't get the NFL — you get a worse NFL, with lecture halls attached, and the belief that filled the stadium drains out on a schedule no one can predict until it's gone.

There is a middle road, and we're standing on its first mile: revenue sharing without employment, athlete representation on governing bodies, guaranteed multi-year scholarships, lifetime health coverage for injuries, collective input formalized through associations rather than unions. Messier than Yonghyuk's clean word — but it pays the athletes while refusing to let the job swallow the school. Some institutions are worth an untidy legal category. I'd rather patch the halfway house than burn it for the honesty of the ashes.

## The Replies

**Choi replies:** Kyusung's middle road has one flaw: every protection on it remains a revocable gift. Associations "consulted" by management are suggestion boxes. And his beloved embeddedness survives employment fine — ask European soccer, where paid players and ferocious local identity have coexisted for a century. Fans adapt to honesty faster than institutions predict.

**Lee replies:** European clubs were never universities — that's the whole point. Yonghyuk keeps proving athletes deserve power and calling it proof they need employment. The strongest labor movements in this country's history built power without waiting for management's category. Let the athletes organize as what they are: the indispensable heart of something bigger than a firm.
