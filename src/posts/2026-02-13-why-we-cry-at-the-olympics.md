It happens every two years, on schedule, to people who consider themselves emotionally composed. A week into the Milan-Cortina Games, my group chats are already full of confessions: cried at a medal ceremony for a sport I cannot explain the rules of; cried at a skater hugging her coach; cried at a forty-year-old crashing out of her last downhill and waving to the crowd anyway. The Winter Olympics is, among other things, a fortnight-long global crying event — and because emotions on a schedule are a psychologist's favorite kind, it's worth asking seriously: what exactly are these tears?

**Borrowed Triumph, Real Chemistry**

The first layer is the most studied. Psychologists call it "basking in reflected glory" — the well-documented tendency to absorb the achievements of groups we identify with as if they were partly ours. Fans of a winning team don't just feel pleased; measurable things happen — mood, confidence, even hormone levels shift with a victory they contributed nothing to. The pronouns give it away: we won, they lost.

The Olympics turbocharges this mechanism by raising the stakes of identification to the largest group most of us belong to: the nation. When Kim Gil-li surged to gold in the short track this week — a result that sent my own family's chat into celebration at ungodly hours — Koreans everywhere experienced something psychologically real: a self temporarily enlarged to the size of a country. The flag on the suit does what no club logo can. It implicates you.

But reflected glory can't be the whole story, because the strangest Olympic tears aren't for our own flag at all. Nobody in my family has any stake in an Italian speed skater or a Norwegian cross-country dynasty — Norway, as usual, is hoarding golds — and the tears come anyway.

**The Compressed Life**

The second layer is narrative, and the Olympics manufactures a very particular kind. Consider the structure of an Olympic moment: a stranger is introduced to you ninety seconds before the most important minutes of their life. A montage supplies the biography — the injury, the funding struggles, the parent in the stands, the four years of 5 a.m. practices. Then you watch, live and unedited, as their entire accumulated existence is compressed into one run, one routine, one race. Triumph and catastrophe sit one slip apart, and everyone — athlete, crowd, you — knows it in real time.

Psychologically, this is a life span compressed into two minutes, and we respond the way we respond to lives: with what researchers call "kama muta" — the sudden warmth-and-tears emotion triggered by witnessing profound connection or devotion. It's the same feeling produced by reunions, weddings, and acts of sacrifice. The Olympic broadcast is, quite literally, engineered to produce it — the montages are not decoration; they are the delivery system. You cry at the stranger's gold because you were handed, minutes earlier, everything that gold costs.

And the crashes move us even more than the wins. Watching a legend's comeback end in a crumpled heap on a mountainside — as the world did this week with Lindsey Vonn's downhill — triggers something beyond sympathy: the recognition that devotion doesn't guarantee the ending, which every adult quietly knows about their own devotions. The athlete who waves from the stretcher is performing, on our behalf, the hardest human trick: dignity without the prize.

**Effervescence, Scheduled**

The third layer is collective. I've written before about how rarely modern life assembles us — how the shared evening has become culture's scarcest resource. The Olympics is one of the last events that synchronizes a planet: hundreds of millions watching the same run within the same hour, feeling the same gasp. Sociology's term is "collective effervescence" — the electric sense of being one body — and its reliable byproduct is tears that arrive not from sadness but from momentary membership in something enormous.

That's also, I think, why Olympic crying intensified in the streaming era rather than fading. The more atomized our ordinary viewing, the more physiologically startling synchrony becomes. The tears are partly withdrawal symptoms from a togetherness we no longer practice.

**In Defense of the Tears**

It's fashionable to debunk all this — to note that the Games are also a billion-dollar machine of sponsorships and geopolitics, that the sentiment is produced and sold. All true; the machine is real, and this column has never met a spectacle it wouldn't audit. This year's Games even come with a poignant structural irony: a Winter Olympics increasingly dependent on artificial snow, staging pastoral alpine mythology inside a warming world. The tears are sponsored. The mountain is half machine.

And yet the emotion underneath is not counterfeit. What the Olympics actually sells is a concentrated dose of things scarce in daily life: visible devotion, unambiguous stakes, strangers made briefly knowable, and a planet watching together. Our tear ducts respond to the concentrate because the ingredients are real, whatever the packaging. When the skater lands it and grabs her coach, when the anthem plays for someone's impossible comeback, when the forty-year-old waves from the snow — the wet eyes in a hundred million living rooms are not being fooled. They're being reminded: this is what caring completely looks like. We so rarely get to watch.

Cry away. It's the most honest thing on television this fortnight.
