Winter in south Georgia is a rumor. It arrives in late December wearing fog, spends January deciding whether to commit, delivers perhaps one honest freeze — the town discussing it for days, pipes wrapped, propane trucks busy — and by late February it is already backing out the door, apologizing. The daffodils come up before Valentine's Day. The pecan trees, wiser, wait. Nobody here has ever made the mistake of trusting a Georgia February.

I have now spent enough winters here to know the routine, which is itself a strange thing to be able to say. There was a time when this place was entirely new to me — when I wrote in these pages as a self-described newcomer, puzzling over monuments and boiled peanuts and the particular way strangers here call you "baby" at the gas station. Somewhere between then and now, without ceremony, the puzzling stopped. The accent I once studied like a text now just sounds like the town talking. This is what belonging actually feels like, I've decided: not a feeling at all, but an absence of translation.

And this winter — my point, eventually — I have been trying to pay attention to all of it, with an urgency I couldn't explain at first and now can. It is the urgency of last times.

I don't want to be dramatic about this; the culture writer in me distrusts drama the way the town distrusts February. But there is a particular season of life — many readers will know it, and the rest will arrive eventually — when you become aware that your ordinary days are quietly converting into memories in real time. The Friday drives past the stadium lights. The Korean church potlucks where the ajummas load your plate as if famine were forecast. The Waffle House at midnight with people who have known your face since it was rounder. None of it is ending, exactly. But you can feel the tense preparing to change — present shifting toward past perfect — the way you can smell rain before it lands.

Psychologists apparently have a term for savoring things because they're ending; my colleague Gawon informs me the Olympics run on it. I've been running on it since Christmas. It makes the ordinary luminous and slightly unbearable. A gas-station biscuit should not be able to make a person emotional. I am here to report that, under the right conditions, it can.

What has surprised me most is what I find myself hoarding. Not the landmarks — the murals, the courthouse, the famous fried chicken — but the connective tissue. The sound of a screen door at my friend's grandmother's house. The church parking lot after the English service, where the second generation lingers in the cold — our mild, apologetic cold — reluctant to end the one hour a week we are all in one place. The particular quality of Georgia light in late afternoon in winter, which is thin and gold and falls on the pines like it's being rationed. I once interviewed a Korean War veteran who told me that what he remembered of home, across seventy years, was not the big things but "the small weathers." I wrote it down at the time because it was beautiful. I understand it now because it is true.

There's a version of this essay that resolves neatly — that says: and so, cherish everything, for all of it is fleeting. I resist that version, partly because it's a greeting card, and partly because it isn't quite what these months have taught me. Constant cherishing is not livable; nobody can hold every biscuit sacred. What last times actually teach, I think, is something more specific: that attention is a decision, and that most of us defer it, assuming the ordinary will sit still until we're ready to look. The ordinary does not sit still. It converts. The screen door becomes a memory of a screen door on its own schedule, without consulting you.

So I have been looking, this winter, on purpose. Taking the long way past the stadium. Saying yes to the potluck. Letting the ajummas load the plate. Writing down the small weathers while they are still weather and not yet memory.

The daffodils are up again, early as always, foolish as always. Spring here doesn't arrive so much as resume, and this one will resume a life that is — I can feel it — gathering itself toward somewhere else. That story is for another essay, a little later, when the tense has finished changing. For now there is February, backing out the door, apologizing, and me on the porch in a jacket I barely need, paying attention at last, learning what the pecan trees always knew: the ones who wait see everything.
