*This is Olivia Kim's first piece as The Argosy's new science and health writer. She takes over a beat previously anchored by Yehee Jung, whose work this column gratefully builds on. — Eds.*

In January, after a year's notice, the United States formally left the World Health Organization — ending nearly eight decades of membership in the body it helped found, and removing, at a stroke, close to a fifth of the organization's funding. A few months earlier, on a different page of the news, the Americas lost something most people didn't know they had: the entire region's official measles-free status, revoked after sustained outbreaks — the first region in the world to eliminate the disease, and now the first to lose that designation.

My first instinct, reading these stories, was the one I'd guess most people had: politics and biology, separate sections of the newspaper. My goal in my first piece for this paper is to convince you they are the same story — because the connection between an empty chair in Geneva and a virus in a kindergarten is not rhetorical. It is mechanical, and the mechanism is worth understanding precisely.

**Disease Surveillance Is a Group Project**

Here's the biology that makes global health cooperation non-optional. Pathogens evolve, travel, and recombine without regard for borders — a virus circulating anywhere is, given modern air travel, a virus incubating everywhere within a day or two. Because of this, the world's defense against outbreaks isn't primarily medicine. It's information moving faster than infection: thousands of laboratories and clinics feeding surveillance networks, so that a strange pneumonia in one city becomes a global alert before it becomes a global disease.

The WHO is the switchboard of that network. It's easy — and sometimes fair — to criticize it as slow, bureaucratic, or overly deferential to powerful members; every institution that requires 194 countries to cooperate accumulates flaws. But the switchboard function is not replaceable by any single nation, however rich, for a simple structural reason: countries share uncomfortable information (an outbreak is always uncomfortable information) with neutral bodies far more readily than with rival powers. Remove the biggest funder and one of the deepest technical benches from that system, and you don't just shrink a budget. You add friction to the one process — early warning — where friction is measured in lives. The seasonal flu vaccine in an American pharmacy, to give one concrete example, is designed each year from strain data gathered through exactly this global network. The network doesn't stop sharing when a country leaves. But the country is no longer in the room where the sharing happens.

**What "Elimination" Actually Means — and How It Un-Happens**

Now the measles half of the story, because the word "elimination" is doing precise technical work that headlines blur. Eliminating a disease from a region doesn't mean it never appears; travelers import measles constantly. It means the disease can no longer sustain transmission chains — every imported spark lands on damp ground and dies out, because enough of the population is immune. For measles, the most contagious human virus we know — one case infects a dozen-plus susceptible people, and the virus hangs in the air of a room for hours after an infected person leaves — the damp-ground threshold is about 95 percent vaccination coverage. Not 80. Not 90. The virus's own biology sets the passing grade, and it grades harshly.

Regional elimination status falls when any member country sustains transmission for a full year — which is what happened after coverage, drifting downward for a decade and shoved further by pandemic-era disruption and organized distrust, slipped below threshold in enough communities. Sparks started landing on dry grass. The chains stopped dying out. A designation that took generations of vaccination campaigns to earn was gone in a bureaucratic instant, because the biology underneath it had quietly eroded years earlier.

That's the property of public health achievements I find most sobering as a student of biology: they are rented, never owned. Herd immunity isn't a monument; it's a muscle, maintained by millions of unremarkable decisions every year, invisible while it works. The diseases, meanwhile, wait with perfect patience. Measles in 2026 is the same virus it was in 1966. Only we have changed.

**The Sentence Both Stories Share**

So here is the single sentence I promised, joining the empty chair to the empty threshold: infectious disease is the one arena where every nation's protection is built partly out of every other nation's behavior — and both stories are about what happens when the world's participants start treating that shared structure as optional. Withdrawal from the network and drift below the threshold are the same decision at different scales: the decision to stop contributing to something that only works if contributed to.

I'm at the beginning of this beat, and I don't pretend to know how either story ends. Rejoining is possible; thresholds can be rebuilt — countries have recovered elimination status before, through patient, unglamorous campaigns. What I'll bring to these pages, I hope, is the habit my favorite biology teachers drilled into me: follow the mechanism. Politics supplies the nouns of these stories. But the verbs — transmit, mutate, immunize, erode — belong to biology, and biology, unlike the news cycle, never loses interest. I'll be watching the verbs.
