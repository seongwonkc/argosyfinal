Every series has to end somewhere, and I always knew this one would end with a question rather than a place. I have taken you, over these two years, to the restaurants and the churches, the Saturday schools and the veterans' halls of Korean Georgia. For the final part, I traveled lighter. I carried one question, and I asked it of everyone — grandmothers and pastors, shop owners and teenagers, the war veteran who taught me about small weathers: **When you came here, what did you carry?**

I meant it literally at first. The literal answers were wonderful.

A rice cooker, over and over — the first answer in almost every household, named with the reverence other cultures reserve for heirloom silver. One woman, who came to Georgia in the 1980s, still owns hers; it no longer works, and she no longer needs it to. "It fed the first ten years," she said, and that was the whole explanation.

Jars. Pepper paste and soybean paste, wrapped in towels against breakage, carried through airports by women who were told American food would be plentiful and understood, correctly, that plentiful and sufficient are different words. One family told me about a grandmother who sewed dried anchovies into a coat lining, defying customs regulations with the serenity of someone who had already survived far worse than customs. The statute of limitations, we agreed, has expired.

Photographs, of course — but specific ones. Not albums; albums were too heavy. A single wedding portrait. A picture of a mother standing before a house that no longer exists. One man carried his father's funeral photo, the formal kind, because, he said, "someone had to keep his face."

A church directory from a congregation in Seoul — carried, its owner admitted, not to contact anyone, but as proof that she had once been known. A dictionary with a broken spine. A student's chemistry textbook, in Korean, kept "to remember I was good at something, before I had to be new at everything."

**The Second Answers**

But here is what happened in nearly every conversation: after the literal answer came a pause, and then the real one. The rice cooker was never just the rice cooker. Ask what people carried, and eventually they tell you about the invisible luggage.

They carried rank, and its loss — the engineer who became a stockroom worker, the teacher who became a seamstress, professionals demoted by geography, carrying their former selves like documents from a country that no longer certifies them. Several wept telling me this, decades later. The American story prefers its immigrants grateful and ascending; the truer story includes this grief, carried quietly, usually forever, usually so the children wouldn't have to see it.

They carried obligation — the remittances, the phone calls timed to Seoul mornings, the weight of being a family's outpost. And they carried a very particular fear, which more than one person described in nearly the same words: the fear of dying somewhere no one could pronounce their name.

And the second generation — my generation — carries the inheritance of all that carrying. The teenagers I interviewed at Korean school, back in the fall, are carrying it now: the language kept "so the door stays open," the achievement that must justify the demotions, the funeral photo that will someday need a keeper who can read the name on it. We carry lighter versions, translated versions. But nobody in this story travels empty-handed.

**What I Carried**

I owe this series one last honesty, because I have asked everyone else for theirs. When I started writing, I framed myself as a newcomer documenting a community. That was true, and it was also a shield. The real reason I knocked on all those doors, I understand now, is that I was assembling my own luggage — collecting, interview by interview, the proof that a person can be carried by a place she wasn't born in. The veteran's small weathers. The pastor's two-language benediction. The ajummas who loaded my plate and asked about my mother. I came to observe what the Koreans of Georgia carry. I leave understanding that, for two years, they carried me.

This is the final part of "Koreans in Georgia," and the series ends the way the journeys it documented always end: not with arrival, exactly, but with the unpacking. Somewhere ahead of me is a new room, a new town, new weathers to learn. I know now what will make the trip. A jar of something my grandmother would recognize. A photograph. The Georgia light in late winter, thin and gold and rationed, folded up somewhere no customs officer will ever find it.

And a question, the only souvenir I trust, ready for whatever strangers are generous to me next: *what did you carry?* Ask it anywhere, I've learned, and the whole human story unpacks itself onto the kitchen table, still smelling faintly of anchovies and home.

*— This series is dedicated, with gratitude, to everyone who opened a door.*
