*Editor's Note: This is the last roundtable for the writers who built this paper. The Argosy continues — next month's masthead will introduce itself properly, and readers have already met much of it — but this particular table, in this particular configuration, convenes for the final time. They set their own house rule for the occasion: no speeches. Ask each other the questions you never got around to. Lightly edited for length and clarity; the laughter has been left where it fell.*

**Minwoo Jung:** So. Three years, a few hundred pieces, seven time zones' worth of deadline complaints, and this is the last time I get to say: welcome to the roundtable. House rule tonight is no speeches, so I'll open with a question instead, to anyone brave enough: what's the piece you didn't write?

**Yonghyuk Choi:** Easy. The one where I admit I cried at a golf tournament. Multiple golf tournaments. The sports business column projects analytical detachment, and the sports business columnist is a man who got misty at a Ryder Cup apology statement.

**Saerom Kim:** The piece I didn't write is the first draft of Part 1 of my series, which was terrible, and which said "as an objective observer" in the opening paragraph. I was never objective. It took me six parts to admit I was writing about myself. My question back to the table: what did you figure out embarrassingly late?

**Anthony Min:** That readers could tell when I didn't understand something. Early on I'd write around the holes with confident vocabulary. The comments always found the holes. Markets are efficient; comment sections, terrifyingly more so.

**Yehee Jung:** Embarrassingly late realization: my best pieces were the ones where I changed my mind while writing. I used to think that meant I'd planned badly. It took two years to understand that was the whole job — the column was the lab notebook, not the press release.

**Jioh Lee:** I've only been at this table since November, so my late realization is more recent: I spent my first two pieces trying to sound like a writer, and my third one just trying to say true things about the place I'm from. Only one of those approaches produced mail from strangers.

**Kyusung Lee:** Same seat, same timeline. What I learned embarrassingly late — like, February — is that "sports and society" isn't a beat, it's a permission slip. The paper let me write about faith and belonging using box scores as the door. I thought I was smuggling. Turns out the editors could see the smuggling and it was, apparently, the point.

**Minwoo Jung:** It was extremely visible smuggling. We approved every shipment.

**Yehee Jung:** My question for the table, since no one has dared ask it aloud in three years: did anyone actually read the group chat all the way through, ever?

**All:** [a silence the transcript cannot do justice to]

**Saerom Kim:** Moving on. Real question, and I want real answers: what does this paper get to claim? When we're old and insufferable about having founded a student newspaper, what's the true sentence we're allowed to say?

**Minwoo Jung:** That we took the world seriously before anyone asked us to. Nobody assigned a group of teenagers to cover ceasefires and central banks and each other's homesickness. We assigned ourselves. That's the whole claim, and I think it's enough.

**Anthony Min:** The true sentence I'd claim is narrower: we showed our work. Wrong predictions left standing, corrections in public, confessions at every year-end table. In an era allergic to being seen learning, we published our learning. I'm proudest of the graveyard of my bad calls.

**Yonghyuk Choi:** I'd claim the roundtable itself. Anyone can publish takes. We practiced disagreeing — on the record, by name, with people we had to keep liking afterward. Do you know how rare that is on the internet we grew up in? My question to close, then, since someone has to ask it: what are you stealing from someone at this table?

**Kyusung Lee:** Saerom's habit of asking one question and then shutting up. It's interview technique and it's also, I've decided, a theology.

**Jioh Lee:** Anthony's rule — "follow the incentives" — which I intend to apply to every institution I attend for the rest of my life, including, prophylactically, this newspaper.

**Yehee Jung:** Minwoo's discipline of separating what we know from what we feel from what we hope. I came for the science; that sentence structure is what I'm leaving with.

**Saerom Kim:** Yonghyuk's conviction that the game is never just the game. It transferred to everything. The potluck is never just the potluck.

**Anthony Min:** Yehee's lab-notebook honesty. If I ever write anything again, the draft will say "here is where I changed my mind" in the actual text.

**Minwoo Jung:** And I'm stealing from the two newest members, which should embarrass the rest of us: Jioh and Kyusung arrived in November and wrote immediately about home — no throat-clearing, no proving-ground pieces. It took me two years of geopolitics to write one honest paragraph about Seoul. The kids, as the saying goes, are faster.

**Minwoo Jung (continuing):** Last order of business. Next month this table belongs to others — you know their names from these pages, and the paper is in alarmingly good hands; the handover letter will do the formalities in June. Spring is doing its usual thing where several of us find out, via envelope, what happens next — so watch this space, as we say. House rules for the final thoughts, one sentence each, no crying. Yonghyuk is exempt from the second rule for medical reasons.

---
### Final Thoughts

**Minwoo Jung:** We were seven strangers in five time zones who decided the world was our beat — and the great surprise of my adolescence is that deciding was all it took.

**Yehee Jung:** Somewhere in three years of deadlines I stopped writing to seem smart and started writing to get it right, and that is the entire education, and it happened here.

**Saerom Kim:** I came to this paper a newcomer documenting a community and leave it having been documented into one — every door I knocked on turned out to be mine.

**Yonghyuk Choi:** [exercising the medical exemption] The box score of this paper reads: three years, one family, no losses that stayed losses.

**Anthony Min:** Best trade I'll ever make: three years of unpaid labor for a lifetime supply of people who tell me when I'm wrong.

**Jioh Lee:** Five months, and I found the thing I'd been between countries looking for — turns out "where are you really from" can be answered with a masthead.

**Kyusung Lee:** Under the lights on Friday, in the pews on Sunday, and around this table on deadline — same congregation, and I got to belong to all three.

*To every reader who argued with us in the comments, corrected our numbers, and came back anyway: this paper was always a conversation, and you were the other half. Keep reading. The Argosy continues. — The founding table*
