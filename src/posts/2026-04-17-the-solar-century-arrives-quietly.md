Some milestones announce themselves — treaties are signed, ribbons are cut, cameras assemble. Others arrive the way dawn does: gradually, unmistakably, and without a press conference. This spring, in the annual data reviews of the world's electricity system, one of the great milestones of the industrial age slipped quietly into the record books.

Renewable energy has overtaken coal as the largest source of electricity on Earth.

Read that again, because the sentence carries more history than its phrasing admits. Coal has anchored the world's power supply since the very beginning of electrification — since before electrification, if you count the steam century it powered first. Every previous energy "transition" in modern history was really an addition: oil joined coal, gas joined oil, the pie grew and nothing ever actually declined. This is different. In 2025, by the leading independent tallies, renewables generated about a third of the world's electricity — a hair more than coal, for the first time in the modern record — and fossil generation as a whole edged into decline even as humanity's total demand surged. The dirtiest fuel is no longer the biggest. The crossover happened not in a treaty hall but in a spreadsheet, which is exactly where civilizational change usually happens.

**How It Happened: The Boring Miracle**

The engine of the crossover is almost comically unglamorous: cost curves. Solar power has spent two decades getting relentlessly cheaper — roughly following a learning curve where every doubling of installed capacity cuts prices by a fifth — until it became, across most of the world, the cheapest electricity humanity has ever produced. Last year alone, solar generation grew by an amount that would have powered entire countries a generation ago; it has been the largest source of new electricity growth for years running, with batteries now following the same plunging curve a decade behind.

I want to insist on the unglamorousness, because it's the actual lesson. The solar century was not won by summits or slogans. It was won by factory engineers shaving cents per watt, by installers learning to mount panels in hours instead of days, by boring procurement auctions in sunny countries discovering that the low bid kept being photovoltaic. Idealism opened the door, but economics walked through it — and economics, unlike idealism, does not get tired, does not lose elections, and does not care who is president anywhere.

That last point deserves its own paragraph this particular spring. The crossover arrived in the same season that the world's second-largest emitter completed its exit from the Paris Agreement and began dismantling its own climate rulebook — developments my colleague Ethan will address in these pages next week. Set the two facts side by side and you get the strangest truth of the energy transition: policy can accelerate or delay it by years, but the cost curves have detached from any single government's reach. Sunlight is not a policy. It's a price, and it keeps falling.

**What the Milestone Doesn't Say**

Column rules: every milestone gets audited. Three honest caveats.

First, electricity is not energy. Power grids are the easiest part of civilization to clean up; steel, cement, aviation, shipping, and heating still run overwhelmingly on fossil fuels, and electricity is only about a fifth of final energy use. We have crossed the first summit of a mountain range, not the range.

Second, the average hides the hours. Renewables lead on annual totals, but the grid must balance every minute, and the sun still sets on schedule. Until storage finishes its own cost-curve descent, fossil plants remain the system's shock absorbers — which is why my beat obsesses over batteries, from the giant desert farms to the one in your basement I wrote about in the fall.

Third, growth is a treadmill. Global electricity demand is surging — electrification of cars and heating, air conditioning in a warming world, and the data-center boom this paper has covered extensively. Clean power must outrun not just coal but the growth itself. Last year it did, barely. That race re-runs annually.

**Writing It Down**

Still. I began this column in October writing about how the grid was quietly reorganizing itself from the bottom up. Six months later comes the news that the top has flipped too, and I notice that nobody threw a parade — no anthem, no ribbon, no monument. The milestone made the trade press and sank beneath the spring's louder emergencies.

So consider this column the small ceremony the moment didn't get. Sometime in 2025, a century and a half of carbon's reign over electricity ended, and the successor was the sky. The kids in classrooms this morning will live most of their lives on the far side of that crossover, and some of them will find it hard to believe the old arrangement ever existed — the way we squint at photographs of cities lit by gas lamps.

History's biggest changes rarely feel like anything on the day. They feel like a Tuesday. It was probably a Tuesday. Write it down anyway.
