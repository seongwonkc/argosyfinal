*Editor's Note: A transition is underway at The Argosy — the founding cohort held its farewell table last month, and a formal handover letter follows in June. This month, the paper's continuing and new writers convene their first roundtable, joined by our new science and health writer, Olivia Kim. They chose their topic by the traditional method: the thing they were already arguing about. Lightly edited for length and clarity.*

**Ethan Yang:** New table, same rules. And we've picked the question our whole generation argues about at lunch: we're doing homework to prepare for careers that AI is reorganizing while we do the homework. The last two years brought layoff waves explicitly citing AI, hiring freezes at the entry level, and — simultaneously — the loudest talent shortages in a decade for people who can actually wield these tools. So, honestly: how is a person our age supposed to plan a working life? Gawon, the psychology of this first, because I think the anxiety itself is doing damage.

**Gawon Ryu:** It measurably is. There's a particular flavor of paralysis I keep seeing — call it forecast fatigue. When every month brings a new authoritative prediction about which careers are doomed, and the predictions contradict each other, people don't update rationally; they either freeze or grab superstitions. And the cruelest part psychologically: the advice generation happened to give us — "learn to code, that's the safe path" — became the first path visibly disrupted. When the safest advice fails first, trust in all advice craters. That's the actual mental-health event, more than any specific job fear.

**Patrick Kim:** Culture desk observation: you can watch the whiplash in real time in what we romanticize. Five years ago the culture's hero was the software prodigy. The emerging folk heroes now are — I'm not joking — electricians, nurses, welders. The hands. There's a whole genre of content about leaving the laptop economy for the trades. Some of it's aesthetic tourism, but the signal underneath is real: the culture is repricing what "can't be automated" and doing it faster than the guidance counselors can update the slideshows.

**David Lee:** The trades content is also, quietly, an energy story — half those videos are people installing heat pumps and solar. But let me bring numbers, because our beat demands it: the same AI build-out shedding office jobs is creating a construction and infrastructure boom — data centers, transmission lines, power plants. The economy isn't deleting work; it's violently relocating it, from screens toward atoms. The honest question isn't "will there be jobs" — it's whether the people losing the screen jobs are anywhere near the atom jobs, geographically or in training. Historically, transitions this fast strand people. That's the part policy has to catch, and isn't catching.

**Olivia Kim:** Since I'm the newest person here, I'll offer the view from the youngest seat, which is strangely calmer. My year never believed in the stable map — we started high school with AI already writing essays. So we never had the rug pulled; we started on bare floor. What I take from biology, though, is a caution for the optimists: in ecosystems, generalists survive disruptions and specialists flourish in stability. Everyone's advice right now is "specialize in what AI can't do" — but nobody actually knows what that set is; the boundary has moved every single year. Betting your whole development on today's boundary is specialist behavior in the middle of a disruption. Biology would tell you to stay a generalist longer than feels comfortable.

**Ethan Yang:** That's genuinely useful framing. Let me force the practical question, because readers our age deserve more than vibes: concretely, what do you do differently tomorrow? I'll start. Mine is: get closer to problems, not tools. Tools are what's churning — the problem list (grid needs building, people need care, things need fixing) is stable for decades. Skill at a tool depreciates; ownership of a problem compounds.

**David Lee:** Learn something about the physical world regardless of your path — how power, buildings, logistics actually work. The next twenty years run through atoms, and atom-literacy is scarce among screen natives. Also: the atom jobs can't be done remotely from three time zones away, which increasingly looks like a feature.

**Patrick Kim:** Build taste and build in public. AI collapsed the cost of producing acceptable work, which means "acceptable" is now worthless and judgment — knowing which of the thousand generated options is good — is the scarce asset. And judgment only develops through reps with an audience. The portfolio beat the résumé already; the discourse just hasn't noticed.

**Gawon Ryu:** Mine is defensive: protect the deep-work muscle. Whatever the future rewards, it won't reward people who can't hold attention on a hard thing for two hours — and that muscle is exactly what the feed economy strip-mines. Also, genuinely: invest in being good at working with people. Every automation wave in history raised the premium on trust.

**Olivia Kim:** And mine, from the bare floor: stay in evidence, not forecasts. Track what's actually happening to actual fields — the way we'd track any experiment — and update small and often, instead of swallowing one big prophecy annually. The predictions have been reliably wrong in both directions. The data has not.

**Ethan Yang:** Problems, atoms, taste, attention, evidence. Honestly, that's a better commencement speech than most of the real ones this spring will get.

---
### Final Thoughts

**Ethan Yang:** Plans are obsolete; planning isn't. Own problems, rent tools, and re-plan annually without panic — that's the whole strategy.

**Gawon Ryu:** The scarcest career resources of the next decade are attention and trust. Both are trainable. Neither is downloadable.

**David Lee:** The work is moving from screens to atoms — to the grid, the hospital, the build site. Meet it partway.

**Patrick Kim:** When everyone can generate, the generator isn't the artist. The chooser is. Develop the chooser.

**Olivia Kim:** Treat your career like an organism in a shifting ecosystem: stay adaptable longer than feels comfortable, and trust observed data over confident prophecy.
