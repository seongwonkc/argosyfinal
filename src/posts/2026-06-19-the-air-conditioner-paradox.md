Europe is melting through the hottest June ever measured on the continent — western Europe's temperature anomaly this month has shattered the record set just last year, and the summer is young. The images are becoming annual liturgy: tourists fainting at ancient monuments, cities opening churches and metros as cooling shelters, wildfire smoke over Iberia. And beneath the headlines, a quieter statistic is doing the real historical work: air conditioner sales across Europe are surging — in countries that spent a century regarding the machine as an American extravagance, vaguely vulgar, unnecessary in a civilized climate.

The civilized climate is gone. And the machine Europeans are now buying by the million is my subject this week, because it is the perfect specimen of the problem my beat orbits endlessly: the technology that is simultaneously the cure and a dose of the disease.

**The Paradox, Stated Precisely**

Here is the loop. Heat waves kill — quietly, at scale, mostly the old, the sick, and the poor; the great European heat waves of this century have each claimed tens of thousands of lives, deaths tallied afterward in mortality statistics rather than on any evening news. Air conditioning is, bluntly, the most effective life-saving device in a severe heat wave; where it's widespread, heat mortality collapses.

But cooling demands electricity precisely when the grid is most strained — the hottest afternoons, everyone's compressor roaring at once — and in much of the world that marginal electricity is still fossil-fired. Add the refrigerants, potent greenhouse gases in their own right when they leak, and the loop closes: warming drives cooling, cooling — as currently powered — feeds warming, which drives more cooling. Roughly a tenth of global electricity already goes to cooling, and the world is projected to add billions of new units in the coming decades as incomes rise across the hottest latitudes. Most of the humans who will ever own an air conditioner haven't bought it yet.

It is fashionable to narrate this as tragedy or hypocrisy. It is neither. A grandmother in Seville buying a unit for a heat wave is not a moral failure; she is responding rationally to a danger she didn't create. That's what makes the paradox worth studying instead of moralizing: it's a system problem, and system problems have system answers.

**Four Exits From the Loop**

The good news — and this column insists on precision about good news — is that the loop has exits, all four already under construction.

First, the machines themselves. The efficiency gap between the best air conditioners on the market and the average one being sold is enormous — the best units deliver several times the cooling per watt of the junk that dominates price-sensitive markets. Closing that gap is pure policy: standards, labeling, bulk procurement. A global efficiency push here is among the cheapest climate wins available anywhere — cooling doubled, emissions flat, and nobody sacrifices a thing except the worst products on the shelf.

Second, the timing. Cooling demand peaks in the afternoon; so, conveniently, does solar generation. No load on the grid pairs more naturally with photovoltaics — the sun causing the demand can increasingly power the response, with batteries (a returning character in this column) bridging the brutal early-evening hours. The solar century I wrote about in April was, among other things, the announcement that summer afternoons are becoming the grid's easiest hours instead of its hardest.

Third, the buildings. The air conditioner is often an apology for architecture — for glass towers and treeless streets and the vernacular wisdom (shade, thermal mass, cross-ventilation, white roofs) that the twentieth century paved over. Passive design, urban trees, and reflective surfaces can shave degrees off a neighborhood before a single compressor starts; cities from Athens to Ahmedabad now employ heat officers who plan for summer the way northern cities plan for snow. The cheapest kilowatt-hour of cooling remains the one a shade tree makes unnecessary.

Fourth, the honesty. Part of the exit is simply admitting cooling is not a luxury — it's infrastructure, like heating, like clean water — and planning accordingly: public cooling centers as standard civic equipment, efficiency subsidies aimed at the households least able to afford the good machines, and grid build-out that assumes, rather than perpetually re-discovering, that summers now arrive armed.

**The Sound of Adaptation**

I'll end where my columns keep ending lately, on the ground. Somewhere in Paris or Milan this week, a family that never owned an air conditioner is installing its first one — a small white box on a centuries-old wall, humming its flat, unromantic note into a courtyard that Napoleon's contemporaries designed for a different planet. Multiply that box by a hundred million and you have the actual sound of climate adaptation: not summits, not slogans — compressors, coming online, one strained afternoon at a time.

Our job — the job of the field this column covers — is to make sure that sound doesn't deepen the heat that summoned it. The engineering exists. The sun is even cooperating. What's left is the assembling, which is, as always on this beat, the whole game. Stay cool out there. That sentence used to be a pleasantry. It's infrastructure policy now.
