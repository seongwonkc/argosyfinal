The joke is older than my parents: fusion is the energy source of the future, and always will be. Thirty years away, forever. Every energy writer inherits the joke with the beat, and every energy writer eventually has to decide what to do with it.

Here's why I'm setting it down this summer. In a hall outside Boston, a company spun out of MIT is assembling a machine called SPARC around a set of magnets that did not exist, anywhere, when the joke was coined: high-temperature superconducting coils producing fields of twenty tesla — several hundred thousand times Earth's magnetic field, the strongest ever built for a fusion device. The first of eighteen went in this winter; the full ring is due in place by summer's end, with the switch-on campaign targeted for next year. And in July, something happened that my beat found more remarkable than any plasma physics: pension funds — the institutions that manage teachers' and firefighters' retirements, the most risk-allergic money on Earth — put a billion dollars into the company. Fusion has been funded by governments, billionaires, and true believers for seventy years. It has never before been funded by actuaries.

**Why the Magnet Changes the Argument**

A one-paragraph physics refresher, honestly earned. Fusion — squeezing light atoms together until they merge and release energy, the sun's own mechanism — requires holding a plasma at a hundred million degrees. Nothing material can touch it, so you cage it in magnetic fields. Here's the beautiful arithmetic at the heart of the current moment: the fusion power you can wring from a given machine scales roughly with the *fourth power* of the magnetic field strength. Double the field, and the same-sized machine gets sixteen times better. That's why the old road to fusion ran through sheer size — the international ITER project in France, cathedral-scaled and decades-budgeted: with the old magnets, only enormity could compensate.

The new superconducting tape rewrote the equation. Fields twice as strong mean a machine a fortieth the volume can, on paper, reach the same milestone — which is exactly SPARC's wager: a tokamak that fits in a warehouse, built by a few hundred people in a few years, designed to produce meaningfully more energy from its plasma than goes in. Not a power plant — SPARC is the demonstration; the power plant, already under development with a utility site in Virginia, comes after. But it would be the proof, at commercial scale and commercial speed, that the joke's premise is dead.

**What the Actuaries Saw**

I keep returning to the pension money, because my beat has taught me to read financing the way sailors read barometers. Venture capital buys stories; billionaires buy legacies; governments buy options. Pension funds buy *probabilities* — long, boring, defensible streams of future value. Their arrival doesn't mean fusion is solved; smart money is regularly wrong, and this summer's energy panic — a strait closed, oil spiking, every government on Earth rediscovering the phrase "energy security" — has made any domestic, fuel-free power source look temporarily irresistible. Discount accordingly.

But note what the bet is actually on, because it's subtler than "fusion works." The wager is that the *sequence* now has acceptable odds: magnets (built — the hardest engineering already bolted to the floor), then plasma (next year's exam), then net energy, then a first plant in the 2030s. Each step has a failure mode; plasmas have humiliated seventy years of confident schedules, and this column will report the humiliations if they come. The honest status is: fusion has moved from "unknown physics" to "brutal engineering with a checklist," and brutal-engineering-with-a-checklist is precisely the category of problem this civilization is best at. That migration between categories is what a billion unexcitable dollars was buying.

**The Part That Isn't About Fusion**

Regular readers know this column's discipline: no miracle passes unaudited. So, the audit. Even on the rosiest schedule, fusion arrives too late to matter for the 2020s and most of the 2030s — the decades the climate math actually cares about. The transition being won right now is being won by the unglamorous champions this column covers all year: solar, which just helped renewables pass coal; batteries; grids; efficiency. Anyone selling fusion as a reason to relax about this decade is selling delay with better physics.

Fusion's real promise sits one horizon out: the 2040s-and-after question of firm, dense, always-on clean power for a civilization that will, by then, have electrified nearly everything and doubled its demand — the baseload beneath the solar century. That's not a consolation prize. It's the difference between a transition that plateaus and one that completes.

The plasma will render its verdict soon enough — first the full ring of magnets this summer, then the switch-on. Seventy years of the future arriving never; now a date on an engineering calendar, and the world's most cautious money in the room, watching. I don't know which way it breaks. I know the joke finally has an expiration date, and on this beat, that alone qualifies as news.

Thirty years away, forever? Check back next year. For once, nobody's laughing.
