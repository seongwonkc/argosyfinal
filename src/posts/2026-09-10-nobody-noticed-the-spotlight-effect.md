There is a particular kind of replay that runs in the head during the first weeks of a school year. The answer in class that came out slightly wrong. The wave returned to someone who was actually waving at the person behind you. The shirt that, somewhere around second period, you decided was a mistake. The replay is vivid, detailed, and runs on a loop well into the evening, and it rests on an assumption so automatic that most of us never stop to examine it: that everyone else saw it too.

Psychology has been testing that assumption for decades, and the results are among the kindest in the field.

**What Did the Barry Manilow Shirt Prove?**

In 2000, the Cornell psychologist Thomas Gilovich and his colleagues Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky published a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology that gave a familiar feeling its name: the spotlight effect, our tendency to overestimate how much attention other people pay to us. The study everyone remembers involved a T-shirt. Undergraduate volunteers put on a shirt bearing a large picture of the singer Barry Manilow, a face pretested as embarrassing for college students to wear, and walked into a room of other students. Afterward, they estimated how many people in the room had noticed the shirt.

Their average guess was about half. The real figure was less than a quarter.

Gilovich has told Cornell's alumni magazine that the paper contains several studies, and that the Manilow one is "the one everybody always talks about." The other studies change what the finding means. Wearers also overestimated how many people noticed when the face on their shirt wasn't embarrassing at all. In another study, students held a group discussion and then estimated how the group had seen their contributions, and they overestimated how much their moments had registered, whether they were recalling the good ones or the cringeworthy ones. So the spotlight isn't really a fear of judgment. It's a miscalculation of attention itself.

**Why Is the Light So Bright From the Inside?**

The explanation the researchers favored is an anchoring problem. You are the one person in the room who cannot stop experiencing your own shirt. When you guess what others noticed, you start from your own intense awareness and adjust downward, and you don't adjust nearly far enough. The tell came from a variation on the shirt study: when wearers waited fifteen minutes before making their estimates, long enough to get used to the shirt, they guessed that fewer people had noticed it. Nothing about the room had changed. Only their own attention had moved on.

That detail is the one I keep returning to, because it means the spotlight was never measuring the room. It was measuring you. And everyone else in that room is running the same miscalculation about themselves, each one the star of a private film, replaying a private wrong answer. The audience you imagine scrutinizing you is mostly busy imagining its own audience.

A former Gilovich student, Nick Epley, later extended the idea to the moments that actually do get noticed. People caught in an embarrassing situation in front of others, he wrote, "consistently overestimated how harshly others were evaluating them." Even when the spotlight finds you, it is dimmer and more forgiving than it feels.

**Is This Just a Teenage Thing?**

Partly, and here the research gets humbler. In 1967, the developmental psychologist David Elkind coined the term "imaginary audience" for the adolescent conviction of being the focus of everyone's attention. Elkind argued that teenagers assume others are as preoccupied with them as they are with themselves, and he expected the feeling to emerge around eleven or twelve and gradually fade. Later studies have questioned that tidy timeline, finding the pattern in younger and older teenagers alike and, in at least one comparison, in young adults about as much as in adolescents. Gilovich's shirt-wearers, remember, were college students.

So I'd put it this way: the spotlight effect isn't something you outgrow on a birthday. It's a feature of having a self, turned up in adolescence because adolescence is when social standing feels most urgent. In August I argued that back-to-school dread is really the anxiety of an expired social map. The spotlight effect is that same anxiety in its most concentrated form, the mind convinced that the whole room is watching while it redraws the map.

**What About When It Goes Well?**

A cousin finding is even more useful. In 2018, the psychologists Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark published five studies in Psychological Science on what they called the "liking gap." In one, strangers had a five-minute conversation built on typical icebreaker questions, then rated how much they liked their partner and how much they thought their partner liked them. On average, people liked their partners more than they believed their partners liked them. Video analysis suggested that people missed the signals of interest and enjoyment their partners were sending. In a study of actual college roommates, the gap lasted for months. Clark's diagnosis was that people are "too wrapped up in their own worries about what they should say or did say."

Side by side, the two findings form a pattern. We overestimate how much others notice our stumbles, and we underestimate how much they enjoy our company. It's the same error running in both directions: attention fixed so firmly on our own performance that we lose track of everyone else's actual experience of us.

**So What Do You Do With the Replay?**

The lesson is not "nobody cares about you." That would be untrue, and unkind besides. The lesson is about where attention actually goes. Other people are paying less attention to your mistakes, and more warmth to you, than the replay insists.

I've come to think the most practical response is to turn the light around. The person next to you in that classroom may well be replaying something of their own tonight, a wrong answer or a misread wave or a shirt they regret. If the shirt study is any guide, you are one of the people who didn't notice it. Which means you are also one of the people who could make the room feel a little less like a stage.

The spotlight is real in the sense that you feel it. It's just pointed the wrong way, and you are allowed to look out from under it.
