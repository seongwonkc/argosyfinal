You have probably heard the claim, maybe from a teacher, maybe from a headline, maybe from the anxious voice that shows up whenever you reach for your phone to look up something you feel you should already know: the internet is ruining our memories. There's even a name for the condition, "digital amnesia," and it sounds like a diagnosis.

It isn't one. As Jonathan Jarry of McGill University's Office for Science and Society has pointed out, the term came not from memory researchers but from a cybersecurity company, Kaspersky Lab, which popularized it through surveys about phone habits and data backups. Jarry called it "an example of bad PR." But the term caught on because it seemed to rest on real science, and in particular on one paper that has been cited, quoted and paraphrased for a decade and a half.

I should say up front that this is exactly the kind of finding I'd want to be true. This column has spent years arguing that media environments shape attention, from doomscrolling to Australia's social media ban. Which is precisely why it deserves the closest look.

**What Did the Famous Study Find?**

In 2011, Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner published "Google Effects on Memory: Cognitive Consequences of Having Information at Our Fingertips" in Science, one of the most prestigious journals in the world. The paper described four experiments. In the first, undergraduates answered trivia questions and then did a color-naming task, in which computer-related words such as "Google" and "browser" slowed their responses more than neutral words did. The researchers took this as a sign that hard questions make us think of computers, the way a hungry person's attention snags on the word "pizza."

In the other experiments, participants typed trivia statements into a computer and were told either that the computer would save them or that it would erase them. Knowing the information would be erased improved memory for it. And when the information was saved, people were better at remembering where it was stored than what it said.

The authors concluded that the internet had become a kind of transactive memory, a partner we split our remembering with, the way people who live together each remember different parts of a shared life. It's an elegant idea, and it spread fast, usually in a darker form: Google is making us forget.

**What Happened When Scientists Checked?**

In 2018, a large international team of researchers, with Colin Camerer as first author, published an attempt to replicate 21 social science experiments that had appeared in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015. Only 13 of the 21, about 62 percent, produced a significant effect in the same direction as the original, and the effects that did reappear were on average about half as large as first reported. Sparrow's first experiment, the color-naming test, was one of the ones that failed.

Sparrow responded that her original procedure had been slightly different, and she had a point about the passage of time: her tests had been run in 2006, with computer-related words that included "Altavista" and "Lycos," search engines that most people reading this have never used. But when a team in Germany ran the first experiment again, they also failed to find the effect.

The memory experiments fared a little better, with an important condition attached. In 2021, a team at the University of California, Santa Cruz, found the effect only when participants first completed a practice round that showed them the computer's saving function was reliable. The original study had no such practice round.

**So Is the Google Effect Fake?**

Not exactly, and this is where I think the story gets more interesting than the headline. What failed is the dramatic version, the one in which search engines quietly rewire us. What survives, conditionally, is something almost obvious once you say it plainly: when we trust a tool to hold information for us, we put less effort into holding it ourselves. And notice the condition the Santa Cruz team found. The effect depended on trust, on people believing the computer would actually keep what they typed.

That's not amnesia. It's a decision, even if we don't experience it as one. Jarry's conclusion was that offloading information onto devices "may be more of an adaptation than a devolution," freeing up attention for things that matter more.

A related line of research makes the same point from another angle. In a 2014 study, the Fairfield University psychologist Linda Henkel led participants on a guided tour of an art museum and asked them to observe some objects and photograph others. Afterward, they remembered fewer of the objects they had photographed whole, and fewer details about them, which Henkel named the "photo-taking impairment effect." But when participants zoomed in to photograph a specific detail, their memory held up. The camera didn't erase anything. What mattered was whether the act of using it involved paying attention. As Henkel put it, "the 'mind's eye' and the camera's eye are not the same."

**Why Did the Scary Version Spread?**

I think the alarming story traveled for the same reason a lot of alarming stories about technology do. It flatters a feeling many of us already have, that something is slipping, and it gives that feeling a villain. A finding that says "people remember less of what they expect to be stored, under certain conditions" doesn't make a headline. "Google is eating your brain" does.

That doesn't make the worry foolish. Jarry is careful to say there may be genuine downsides to letting technology remember for us, and that we need more and better studies. But the honest summary of the evidence today is far narrower than the one we absorbed.

**What Should You Actually Do With This?**

Treat offloading as a choice, because that is what the research suggests it is. A phone number, a bus schedule, the release date of a movie: let the phone carry them, and spend the attention elsewhere. But for the things you need inside your own head, the vocabulary for Thursday's quiz, the formula, the argument you're building for an essay, the save button may work against you. If you trust the notes to remember, your brain may happily agree to let them.

The internet didn't steal your memory. It offered to carry some of it, and the deal mostly works. The trick is remembering which things you handed over, and which ones you meant to keep.
