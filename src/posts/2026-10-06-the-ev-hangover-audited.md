On September 30 of last year, a car dealer's favorite deadline arrived. The federal tax credit for electric vehicles, worth up to $7,500 on a new one and up to $4,000 on a used one, ended that day, ahead of its original schedule, cut short by the tax and spending package Congress passed in early July 2025. In the final weeks, buyers stampeded. "EV sales just exploded," Matt Jones of TrueCar told NPR on deadline day. "It's been bonkers." Battery electric cars reached a record 12 percent of new light-duty vehicle sales that September, according to the Energy Information Administration.

In the same NPR story, an Edmunds analyst named Ivan Drury offered the sentence that has hung over the industry ever since: "The end of the tax credit created a rush in September, but it could also trigger an EV hangover in the months ahead."

A year later, most of the numbers are in, and this column's rule is to audit them.

Start with the hangover, because it was real. In the second quarter of 2026, Kelley Blue Book estimates that Americans bought about 247,000 electric vehicles, down 20.5 percent from the same quarter a year earlier. Battery electric cars fell to 6 percent of new light-duty sales, down from 7 percent a year before and half of last September's peak. Plug-in hybrids slipped from 1.9 percent of sales to 1.4.

Before the deadline, the research firm Rhodium Group estimated that killing the credit early would cut EV sales by 16 to 38 percent compared with where they otherwise would have been. That is a different measurement from a year-over-year drop, so the two numbers do not line up neatly. But a 20 percent fall lands squarely inside the range the forecasters drew. Anyone telling you the credit never mattered should explain where those cars went.

But a hangover is not a collapse. Second-quarter EV sales were up 14.7 percent from the first quarter, which means the market found its floor early and started climbing off it. Tesla still sold about half of all the EVs in the country. Toyota's EV sales grew 225 percent from a year earlier. Subaru's more than doubled. The brands that were late to electric cars are, a little awkwardly, arriving just as the subsidy left.

And on October 2, Tesla reported its third-quarter deliveries: 486,532 vehicles worldwide, beating the 461,974 that analysts had expected. That was only about 2 percent below the same quarter last year, which was Tesla's all-time record, inflated by the American deadline rush. The numbers are global, so they say little about America on their own. Still, a company that was supposed to be facing a cliff posted something closer to a dip.

The biggest story of the year is a car that plugs into nothing. In the second quarter, conventional hybrids, the kind that pair a gas engine with a small battery and never touch a charger, reached a record 16 percent of new vehicle sales. Counting hybrids, plug-ins, and full EVs together, electrified vehicles rose to 24 percent of sales, up from 22 percent a year earlier. Buyers did not abandon batteries when the credit disappeared. Many of them just chose a smaller one.

Then there is the gas pump. AAA reported that the national average for regular gasoline in September was $4.33 a gallon, fifty cents higher than the previous September record, set in 2023, with crude oil hovering around $90 a barrel. Electrek, reporting on Tesla's quarter, wrote that high gas prices are offsetting much of the demand lost when the credit expired. I would hold that claim loosely until the full third-quarter data arrives, but it makes sense. A subsidy is a one-time discount. A fuel price is a bill that arrives every week, and this year it has been a loud one.

**The fine print**

Three items.

The luxury market moved the most. EIA data shows battery electric cars fell from 22 percent of luxury vehicle sales in the second quarter of 2025 to 14 percent in the same quarter this year. That is a far steeper fall than in the market as a whole, which suggests the end of the credit hit pricier electric cars hardest.

The used-car credit is the loss I worry about most. Up to $4,000 off a used EV was the part of the program that reached buyers who were never going to shop for a new car. Its absence will not show up in new-car sales reports, which is exactly why it deserves a sentence here.

And charging is not free just because gas is expensive. AAA's own figures put the national average for public EV charging at 42 cents per kilowatt-hour. A family with a driveway and a wall charger mostly pays home electricity rates. A family in an apartment pays the public price. The EV math has always been friendliest to people who own a garage, and the end of the credit raised the entry price for everyone, garage or not.

The big picture is smaller than either side wanted. Electric vehicles were only about 2 percent of all the cars registered on American roads in 2024, so the fleet turns over slowly no matter what any single year does. A year in, the data fits neither an obituary nor a shrug. The market took a real hit, found a floor, and shifted toward hybrids while the price of gasoline did some of the persuading the tax code used to do.

A year ago, the deadline rush filled dealer lots with buyers in a hurry. This year, a record share of the cars leaving them carry a small battery and a gas tank. That is not the transition anyone drew on a whiteboard. It is the one actually happening, one sales report at a time.
