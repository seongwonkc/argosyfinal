Every October I get to watch the one scoreboard that never grades a keynote: the Nobel Prizes, the slowest judges in science. They rarely reward a promise; they reward things that kept working for decades. This week's three science prizes fit the pattern, each with its own lesson about what counts as proof.

**Physics: The Telescope Nobody Thought Would Work**

The physics prize went to a single person, Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin-Madison, "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin." It is the first solo physics prize since 1992.

Neutrinos are the least sociable particles in the universe: no charge, almost no mass, trillions passing through your body every second. To catch the rare ones that do interact, Halzen proposed in 1988 using the Antarctic ice itself as a detector. The finished instrument, completed in 2010, is a cubic kilometer of South Pole ice threaded with 5,160 light sensors up to 2,450 meters down, waiting for the faint flash of a neutrino collision.

Halzen, now 82, was candid about the odds: "People always thought this was a cute idea, right, but it would never work." It worked. By 2013, two years into full operation, IceCube had recorded 28 high-energy neutrinos from deep space.

Then came my favorite detail. The planned successor, an eight-cubic-kilometer IceCube-Gen2, was put on hold by the National Science Foundation in 2023. Halzen, who is working on a proposal, said, "I hope that this prize will help getting it approved." A laureate, still writing grant applications. Science scored honestly has rounds, not a finish line.

**Medicine: A Light Switch Borrowed From Algae**

The medicine prize went to Karl Deisseroth of Stanford, Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University in Berlin, and Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg "for discoveries of light-gated ion channels and optogenetics." It begins with Chlamydomonas, a single-celled alga that swims toward light. Hegemann's lab identified channelrhodopsin, a protein that opens a channel in the cell when light hits it; Nagel's team then showed that a second version, channelrhodopsin-2, worked differently. In 2005, Deisseroth's lab put that gene into rat neurons and fired them with flashes of blue light, timed to the millisecond.

This prize rewards a tool, not a cure. Committee chair Per Svenningsson said optogenetics offers "opportunities for mapping the brain in a way that we could once only dream of." For two decades, labs have used it to ask causal questions: switch on these neurons, and does a sleeping animal wake up? Bedside uses, like restoring light sensitivity to a failing retina, have been shown in mouse models of blindness. Treatment is a separate exam; the instrument has passed its own.

**Chemistry: A Reaction That Picks a Hand**

Chemistry went to Henri Kagan of Université Paris-Sud and Kenso Soai of the Tokyo University of Science "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis." Translation: many molecules come in left- and right-handed mirror-image forms. Life is picky, building proteins only from left-handed amino acids, while ordinary lab reactions tend to produce a fifty-fifty mix. In the 1980s Kagan showed that a catalyst could make a product purer in one hand than the catalyst itself. In 1995 Soai described a reaction whose product speeds up its own formation. Starting from symmetric ingredients, he ran it 37 times: 19 runs favored the left-handed form, 18 the right, and not one came out even.

That tally is my number of the week. Committee member Peter Somfai called it "probably the coolest experiment in organic chemistry," and it is the cleanest scoreboard I have seen: a coin that never once landed on its edge. It offers one possible route to how life's molecules all ended up the same hand.

**The Common Thread**

Three prizes, three instruments: a detector, a switch, a reaction. Each was a working method that strangers could pick up and use for years. That is the bar I hold every product launch to, and it is reassuring to see Stockholm holding it too. October's scoreboard: ice 1, light 1, mirrors 1, hype 0.
