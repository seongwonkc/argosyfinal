On Tuesday, the Television Academy and Amazon announced a six-year deal that will move the Emmy Awards to Prime Video, starting with next September's ceremony. The Emmys have aired on one of the big broadcast networks every year since 1955; for the past three decades they rotated among ABC, CBS, Fox, and NBC like a family passing around holiday hosting duties. That rotation is over. The awards show that exists to celebrate television is leaving television, or at least the version of television that came with an antenna.

The Academy, to its credit, did not pretend this was a tragedy. As its chairman, Cris Abrego, put it in announcing the deal, the Academy "has never defined television by how it reaches your screen." That's a graceful line. It's also, as a matter of cultural history, a polite way of saying the screen stopped being the point a long time ago.

**A Show About Television, Leaving Television**

The Oscars made the same move first. Last December, the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences announced that the Academy Awards will leave ABC, their home since 1976, for YouTube in 2029. The Emmys simply skipped the waiting period. Two of the biggest awards nights in American entertainment are now officially headed for streaming, and nobody marched in the streets about either one.

That calm is the real story. Twenty years ago, a decision like this would have been framed as the end of an era. Today it reads like a change of address. The reason is simple: the era already ended, and the paperwork is just catching up. AP's account of the deal noted that the Emmys once drew more than 20 million viewers and now hover around 7 million. The Oscars peaked at 57 million in 1998 and drew 19.7 million last year. These are still large audiences. They are not the country.

**The Arithmetic of a Monday**

Consider the ceremony we just had. The 78th Emmys aired on September 14 on NBC and Peacock, and they were shoved from their traditional Sunday to a Monday so that NBC could air Sunday Night Football. On Monday, the show was promptly outrated by the season's first Monday Night Football game anyway. Preliminary figures put the Emmys at about 6.7 million viewers, down from roughly 7.6 million the year before. Regular readers know my position on football's relationship to the rest of culture: the game gets the good night, and everything else gets the leftovers.

Now hold that 6.7 million next to a different number. In May, *The Late Show with Stephen Colbert* aired its final episode, after CBS canceled the program in what the network called a financial decision. That finale drew 6.74 million viewers, the most-watched weeknight episode in the show's eleven-year history, for a show that had been averaging about 2.7 million viewers a night earlier in the year. In other words, the goodbye of a single late-night program drew roughly as many people as the whole of television's annual celebration of itself. Four months later, that same canceled show won the Emmy for Outstanding Variety Series. Even the trophies are arriving after the audience has gone home.

**What Amazon Is Actually Buying**

So why would anyone pay for a ceremony with a shrinking audience? Here the column becomes an audit, as these columns usually do.

Amazon is not buying viewers, exactly. It's buying a ritual. Streaming services are extraordinary at giving each of us exactly what we want, alone, whenever we want it. What they struggle to manufacture is the *appointment*: the thing everyone has to watch at the same time or risk having it spoiled at lunch. Live sports are the obvious example. Awards shows are a smaller version of the same thing: one live night, one shared red carpet, one set of winners debated the next morning. The Emmys will remain free to watch, with no subscription required. That's not charity. That's a front door. The goal is to have one of the year's few shared nights happen inside your store.

There's also a quieter truth in the ratings data. The 2026 Emmys racked up 133 million video views across social platforms, up 90 percent from last year. The ceremony itself is shrinking; the clips are exploding. The awards show has become a clip factory with a three-hour production schedule, and most of its "audience" never watches the factory, only what rolls off the line. A streamer that owns the factory, the replays, and the recommendation engine is in a better position to cash those clips in than a broadcast network that just airs the thing once and hopes.

**The Antenna in the Attic**

I wrote last fall that the monoculture died of a plumbing change: infinite pipes, no chokepoint, no night when a hundred million people face the same direction. The broadcast networks were the last of the old pipes, and the awards shows were among the last tenants still living in them. This week, one of those tenants packed up.

I'm not inclined to mourn. A ceremony that celebrates *The Pitt*, a streaming hospital drama, and *Widow's Bay*, a streaming comedy, probably belongs where its winners live.

But I'll note what's being lost, because nobody else seems to be noting it. Broadcast was the last medium where the shared night was the default, where you could stumble onto the Emmys while flipping channels and accidentally participate in a national conversation. On a streamer, nobody stumbles onto anything. You go there on purpose, or the algorithm sends you, or you catch the clip on Tuesday. The audience can still assemble. It just has to be invited now, one notification at a time.

Television's biggest night is moving to the internet. Which is to say: it's moving to where television already went, and where the rest of us were already waiting. The antenna goes in the attic. Somebody should at least wave goodbye.
