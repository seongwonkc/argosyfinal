Students are reading less, and it shows in their test scores and in what they say about reading.

In the United States, about 40 percent of fourth graders scored below the Basic level on the 2024 national reading test, the largest share since 2002, according to the [National Assessment Governing Board](https://www.nagb.gov/news-and-events/news-releases/2025/nations-report-card-decline-in-reading-progress-in-math.html). About a third of eighth graders fell below Basic, the largest share the test has ever recorded. Average scores in both grades dropped two points from 2022, after dropping three points from 2019.

Surveys outside the U.S. find the same slide in how students feel about reading. In the United Kingdom, the [National Literacy Trust](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-reading-in-2025) asked young people in early 2025 whether they enjoy reading in their free time. Only 32.7 percent of 8- to 18-year-olds said yes, a 36 percent drop since the charity first asked in 2005. Fewer than one in five said they read something every day outside school.

So what gets students to read more? One of the clearest answers comes from a study that did very little teaching at all.

## Twelve books and a book fair

Researchers led by Richard Allington and Anne McGill-Franzen worked with first and second graders at 17 high-poverty elementary schools in two Florida school districts. Some students were picked at random for the program. Each spring, those students went to a book fair with 400 to 600 titles and chose 15 they wanted. On the last day of school, they took 12 of them home to keep.

That was the whole program. No summer school, no tutors and no reading logs. The students got books they had chosen for themselves, three summers in a row.

By the end of the third summer, most of the students were in fourth or fifth grade. The group that got the books scored higher on Florida's state reading test than the group that didn't. A [review by the What Works Clearinghouse](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/QuickReview/sumread_092910.pdf), part of the U.S. Department of Education, found the study met its evidence standards. It put the size of the gain at roughly moving a student from the 50th to the 56th percentile. For the poorest students, those eligible for free lunch, the gain was larger.

Six percentiles is a modest change. It came from a dozen paperbacks a year, handed out on the day when students were about to lose a long stretch of school.

## What schools can take from it

The study tested one thing, so it can't say which part mattered most: the choosing, the owning or the timing. Each one points to something a school or a parent can try.

**Let students choose.** In the Florida study, students picked their own books from hundreds of options. Nobody assigned them. Class reading lists still have a job to do, but a summer stack should hold books a student already wants to open.

**Put the books in their hands.** The students kept the books. A book at home, on the bedside table, gets picked up in a way that a library book due back in two weeks may not. Schools that can't afford new books can still run swaps, where every student brings one and leaves with another.

**Start before summer.** The books arrived on the last day of school. The weeks without class are when reading habits slip, and the study placed the books right at that moment.

## A habit, not a score

The test scores are the part researchers can measure. The National Literacy Trust's numbers point to the part that's harder to measure: whether students want to read when no one is checking.

A student who picks a book, owns it and has time for it has a reason to open it. Getting students to read more may come down to giving them that reason, one self-chosen book at a time.
