A Nomura's jellyfish has no brain, no heart, and no passport. It needs none of them to cross the Yellow Sea.

Editors are allowed one indulgence, and this column is mine. The sustainability desk is in David's hands now, but I have been reading about one animal all year, and I want to explain why it keeps me up. The jellyfish itself is the smaller reason. The bigger one is the people who watch for it.

**The Drift**

The giant jellyfish starts small. Its seeding ground is [Chinese coastal waters](https://www.ices.dk/sites/pub/CM%20Doccuments/CM-2014/Theme%20Session%20A%20contributions/A0114.pdf), where young medusae grow before currents carry them north and east; scientists still [argue over exactly where the nursery is](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2022.1098232/pdf), with the Yangtze estuary the leading suspect. In the great bloom of 2005, Korean government scientists [tracked them](https://meetings.pices.int/publications/presentations/PICES_15/Ann15_S2/S2_Yang.pdf) appearing near Jeju in early July and spreading through Korea's West and South seas by August. By then they are not small. One Busan fisher [described hauling up](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/fishers-beachgoers-feel-the-sting-as-giant-jellyfish-threat-grows/11114262) jellyfish two meters across and over 150 kilograms.

Picture that in a net built for anchovy.

**What a Bloom Costs**

The damage is mundane, which is why it is easy to miss. The jellyfish [rip nets, double the time crews spend sorting fish from jellyfish, and sting the catch](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/fishers-beachgoers-feel-the-sting-as-giant-jellyfish-threat-grows/11114262) badly enough to lower its price. Korean researchers who [put numbers on it](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12562-012-0533-1) found catches falling between 6.5 and 33.7 percent depending on the fishery, and estimated the annual direct damage at between US$68.2 million and US$204.6 million. Japan's [2005 bloom alone](https://www.ices.dk/sites/pub/CM%20Doccuments/CM-2014/Theme%20Session%20A%20contributions/A0114.pdf) cost its fisheries roughly €220 million.

The ledger, as I keep it. The win: this is a forecastable problem. The cost: the forecast depends on seeing water that belongs to someone else. The catch: the people who pay for the bloom and the people who could see it coming are on different coasts.

**Watching Someone Else's Water**

Japan has already shown that early sight pays. Researchers there learned to forecast a bad year by [counting young medusae in Chinese waters from ships of opportunity](https://www.ices.dk/sites/pub/CM%20Doccuments/CM-2014/Theme%20Session%20A%20contributions/A0114.pdf), ordinary vessels that happened to be passing through. In July 2009, that monitoring triggered a national jellyfish warning, fishermen rigged their nets in advance, and the year's losses came in at about a third of 2005's.

Korea built something different. In 2006 the government set up the Korean Jellyfish Monitoring Network, and its [first watchers](https://archive.iwlearn.net/yslmep.org/yslmep.org/www.yslmep.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Deliverable-14-Jellyfish-Monitoring-Program-Combined-20191115.pdf) were 271 volunteer fishermen, reporting sightings every week from May to December alongside public officials and researchers. By 2015, 146 fishermen's associations had joined. Their reports feed the jellyfish advisories that tell those same fishermen when to pull their gear.

But the bloom crosses a border, and the data mostly stops at it. In a 2019 report for the Yellow Sea's regional environmental program, Korean researchers wrote that confirming where the jellyfish come from requires cooperative research and data sharing with Chinese scientists, which they called "[absolutely required](https://archive.iwlearn.net/yslmep.org/yslmep.org/www.yslmep.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Deliverable-14-Jellyfish-Monitoring-Program-Combined-20191115.pdf)." Required, in other words, and not yet routine.

**Ostrom's Rules for Watching**

The economist Elinor Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel for showing [how common property can be successfully managed by user associations](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/press-release/), drawing on fish stocks, pastures, forests, and groundwater basins around the world. The standard view held that a shared resource had to be privatized or run from above. Ostrom went and looked, and found users who governed their resources better than the theory predicted.

From those cases she drew a short list of [design principles](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.3.641) that durable commons tend to share. Two matter here. Monitoring works when it is done by the users themselves, or by people accountable to them. And local institutions last when they are nested inside larger ones that recognize them.

Korea's network passes the first test almost perfectly. The fisherman who reports a bloom is the fisherman whose net it will tear; the watching pays the watcher. The Yellow Sea fails the second. There are national networks on each coast, and a sea between them with no layer above that both sides answer to. That is my reading, not Ostrom's, but her framework predicts exactly this kind of trouble: a resource bigger than any institution watching it.

**The Volunteer Problem**

Which leaves the question I actually care about. Ostrom's monitors have a stake in the harvest. A student in Seoul does not. Neither does a retiree in Daejeon or an office worker in Incheon who has never set a net. So why would anyone far from the coast spend an evening logging a jellyfish?

That question is why I started JellyWatch. My working theory borrows from Ostrom anyway. The fisherman keeps reporting because he sees what his report does: an advisory goes out, he pulls his gear, his net survives. An inland volunteer whose sighting disappears into a spreadsheet gets none of that. If citizen science is going to watch a sea no government sees whole, it has to close the same loop for people with no net in the water.

The jellyfish will cross the Yellow Sea again next summer whether anyone is counting or not. They have never needed a passport. The data, it turns out, does.
